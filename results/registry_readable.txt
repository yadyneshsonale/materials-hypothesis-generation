HYPOTHESIS: At 2D semiconductor/electrode junctions such as CNGS/MoTe2, engineered point defects should be spatially patterned to create a compliant, high-exchange-current interface only in regions of high mechanical conformity, while defect-poor zones are maintained near rough or weakly adhered regions; this should lower parasitic resistance without triggering stress-driven localized degradation.

MECHANISM: Interfacial defects can improve local wetting/contact kinetics and possibly exchange current, but the deposition/stability literature shows that when interfacial resistance becomes low and i/i0 decreases, mechanical stress becomes the dominant controller of current distribution near roughness. Therefore, uniformly making the whole interface more kinetically active is not always stabilizing. If defect-enhanced kinetics are confined to well-adhered, smooth-contact regions, current can be steered away from rough, weak-adhesion protrusions where stress would otherwise concentrate current and accelerate failure, while also reducing overall series resistance at the junction.

CROSSES BOUNDARY: Non-obvious because it merges photovoltaic 2D heterojunction defect management at CNGS/MoTe2 with solid-state-battery stress/current-distribution scaling, recasting defect passivation as a spatially selective electro-mechanical design variable rather than a uniformly minimized quantity.

MECHANISM: Defect engineering in graphene-like 2D materials can tune interfacial friction and wettability; if these defects are introduced in a controlled texture, they can increase interfacial shear resistance and stabilize contact under load. A defect texture that both binds/disrupts mobile adlayers and increases frictional pinning should limit slip, preserve intimate contact, and stop roughness from evolving into stress concentrators that focus current at protrusions.
