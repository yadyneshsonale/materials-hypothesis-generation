## The research question

> For nanoparticles composed of five or more metals in equal or near-equal atomic ratios and initially crystallized as a solid solution, what governs whether structural and chemical stability is maintained across catalytic and thermoelectric operating ranges of 473–1923 K? Formulate one testable mechanistic hypothesis, specify a controlled comparison, predict observations relative to the initial baseline, define the conditions under which the prediction should hold, identify supporting evidence, and state a result that would falsify the hypothesis.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Equiatomic AuCoCuNiPt nanoparticles will not retain their initial high-entropy solid-solution structure during heating because atomic transport will be governed by kinetically active, non-sluggish diffusion rather than entropy stabilization. Above about 670 K, surface-energy differences should drive Au and Cu toward the surface, where they will form an increasingly unstable shell that may melt and evaporate near 970 K.
- **Starting material:** The starting material will be sub-10 nm, equiatomic AuCoCuNiPt high-entropy alloy nanoparticles synthesized by pulsed laser deposition at about 870 K on a silicon nitride membrane. The particles will initially be FCC solid solutions with multi-twinned or icosahedral structures.
- **What is changed or studied:** The nanoparticles will be heated in situ from approximately 300 to 970 K and evaluated by aberration-corrected STEM-EDS together with atomistic molecular dynamics simulations. Temperature-dependent behavior will be compared with the initial particle population and between icosahedral and truncated-octahedral particle models.
- **Conditions:** Heating will occur under vacuum, with comparisons near 470, 670, 770, 870, and 970 K. The hypothesis will concern sub-10 nm nanoparticles over this experimentally accessible interval.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The principal mediator will be active atomic diffusion coupled to shape evolution. Surface-energy minimization should enrich low-surface-energy Au and Cu at the particle exterior, while coalescence or an elliptical-to-spherical transition should locally enhance diffusion.
- **Mechanism:** Au and Cu should diffuse outward as temperature rises, producing a surface-enriched shell. Reshaping should accelerate local transport, after which the segregated shell should become susceptible to melting and evaporation. Stability will therefore be kinetically and morphologically controlled rather than guaranteed by configurational entropy.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to the initial state, particles of roughly 2–4 nm should progressively disappear, the size distribution should broaden, and elliptical particles should become more spherical. Au/Cu loss near 970 K should reduce characteristic particle size by approximately 20%, despite coalescence within parts of the population.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The competing baseline predicts sluggish diffusion and entropy-driven thermal stabilization, with the initial composition, size distribution, and FCC morphology remaining largely preserved during heating.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Substantial instability is expected above roughly 670 K and should depend on particle size and shape. The prediction is restricted to sub-10 nm AuCoCuNiPt particles under vacuum up to about 970 K and should not be extrapolated to bulk alloys, other atmospheres, or the full range to 1920 K.
- **Main uncertainty:** The onset and magnitude of segregation may vary with initial particle shape because icosahedral and truncated-octahedral geometries could provide different diffusion pathways.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Heat initially compositionally homogeneous nanoparticles through the specified temperature steps while tracking matched particle shapes, and compare the trajectories with molecular dynamics predictions for Au self-diffusion.
  - *Measurements:* Au self-diffusivity as a function of temperature; Surface Au and Cu concentration from STEM-EDS; Particle shape, size distribution, and loss of 2–4 nm particles
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if Au diffusivity remains sluggish above about 670 K and no coupled increase in surface Au/Cu enrichment, spherical reshaping, or approximately 20% size reduction occurs near 970 K.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The structural and chemical stability of nanoparticles composed of five or more metals in equal or near-equal atomic ratios is governed by the competition between entropic stabilization of the solid solution and enthalpic driving forces for phase separation or ordering.
- **Starting material:** Nanoparticles composed of five or more metals in equal or near-equal atomic ratios, initially crystallized as a solid solution
- **What is changed or studied:** Variation of temperature across catalytic and thermoelectric operating ranges of 473–1923 K
- **Conditions:** Temperature range of 473–1923 K, equal or near-equal atomic ratios of five or more metals
- **Structure through which it acts:** Competition between entropic stabilization and enthalpic driving forces
- **Mechanism:** Entropy-driven stabilization of the solid solution versus enthalpy-driven phase separation or ordering
- **Predicted outcome:** Maintenance of structural and chemical stability across the operating temperature range, with possible deviations at high temperatures due to increased enthalpic driving forces
- **Compared with (baseline):** Initial crystalline solid solution state
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range of 473–1923 K, equal or near-equal atomic ratios of five or more metals
- **Main uncertainty:** Insufficient data on specific nanoparticle compositions and temperature-dependent phase behavior
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* In situ high-temperature X-ray diffraction and spectroscopy measurements of nanoparticle structure and composition
  - *Measurements:* X-ray diffraction patterns; X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES)
  - *Falsified if:* Observation of significant phase separation or ordering at temperatures below 1273 K would falsify the hypothesis

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The stability of equimolar, five‐or-more metal nanoparticles maintained as a single-phase solid solution is governed by a balance between configurational mixing entropy and differential atomic diffusion kinetics. At lower operating temperatures (≈473 K), the high mixing entropy kinetically suppresses atomic rearrangements, maintaining structural and chemical homogeneity. However, at higher temperatures (up to 1923 K), increased atomic mobility can overcome this entropy stabilization, leading to segregation of elements with lower diffusion activation energies. Thus, nanoparticles whose constituent elements have significantly different diffusion barriers will show a temperature‐driven onset of phase segregation or surface reconstruction (e.g., formation of intermetallics or oxides) relative to the pristine state.
- **Starting material:** Nanoparticles composed of five or more metals in equal or near-equal atomic ratios, crystallized as a homogeneous solid-solution phase.
- **What is changed or studied:** Thermal treatment across the catalytic and thermoelectric operating temperature range (473–1923 K) under controlled (e.g., inert or reducing) atmospheric conditions.
- **Conditions:** Experiments should be conducted in environments free of extraneous reactive species (e.g., oxygen, moisture) unless the role of surface oxidation is under investigation. The nanoparticles should be exposed to stable temperatures at the lower end (≈473 K) and high temperatures (approaching 1923 K) for sufficient durations to allow atomic rearrangements.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Atomic diffusion kinetics (differential diffusion activation energies among the constituent metals) mediate the transition from a stabilized solid solution to a segregated or reconstructed phase.
- **Mechanism:** At lower temperatures, high configurational entropy maintains a metastable, homogeneous phase despite minor differences in atomic size and bonding. At elevated temperatures, increased atomic mobility enables elements with lower diffusion barriers to migrate and segregate, thereby compromising structural and chemical stability. This competition between entropy-driven stabilization and kinetic diffusion limitations governs the operational stability range.
- **Predicted outcome:** Under thermal treatment at lower temperatures (~473 K), the nanoparticles will retain a homogeneous solid-solution structure with minimal local compositional fluctuations. In contrast, when heated toward 1923 K, measurable elemental segregation (e.g., via the formation of discrete domains or intermetallic compounds) or surface reconstruction (e.g., oxide formation if conditions allow) is expected relative to the initial state.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The as-synthesized, homogeneous solid solution nanoparticles serving as the starting point for all thermal treatments.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** This effect is expected to hold for nanoparticles where the size, composition, and surface characteristics are controlled so that the dominant variation is the differential diffusion properties of the constituent metals. Deviations might occur in systems with strong surface energy effects or in reactive atmospheres.
- **Main uncertainty:** The specific onset temperature at which diffusion-driven segregation becomes significant is uncertain and may vary with particle size, exact compositional ratios, and environmental conditions. In addition, surface effects (e.g., oxidation) may either inhibit or accelerate the segregation process, complicating a direct correlation solely to bulk diffusion kinetics.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare a batch of equimolar, five-or-more metal nanoparticles in a solid solution and subject them to controlled thermal annealing experiments at 473 K and 1923 K under inert atmospheres. Use in situ high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping to monitor atomic distributions in real time. Compare these observations to a control sample maintained at room temperature.
  - *Measurements:* Atomic-scale imaging for detection of phase boundaries or segregation.; EDS maps to quantify elemental distributions before and after thermal treatment.; X-ray diffraction (XRD) to detect the formation of secondary phases or changes in lattice parameters indicative of segregation.
  - *Falsified if:* If, after prolonged annealing at 1923 K, the nanoparticles continue to exhibit a homogeneous single-phase structure with no significant elemental segregation or formation of intermetallic/oxide phases—despite clear differences in diffusion activation energies among the constituent elements—then the hypothesis that differential atomic diffusion governs the stability across the temperature range will be falsified.
