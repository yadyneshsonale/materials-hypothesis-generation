## The research question

> For as-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi-type high-entropy alloys in the form of nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons, what testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain what governs their magnetocaloric response and hysteresis during low-applied-field magnetic-cooling cycles, given that the notable response reported for bulk forms may not persist in a genuinely low-dimensional state and that heat-transfer rate limits cycle frequency? Specify one controlled intervention, predicted observations relative to the as-deposited baseline, applicable operating boundaries, supporting prior evidence, and a clear falsification result.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** In as-deposited, non‐equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi high-entropy alloy thin films/nanoribbons, the magnetocaloric response and hysteresis during low-applied-field cycles are predominantly governed by local interfacial strain and defect-induced anisotropies that emerge from rapid, low-dimensional deposition. These stress fields and microstructural imperfections modulate the magnetic phase transitions and heat dissipation rates, thus altering both the magnetocaloric effect and the magnetic hysteresis compared to bulk forms.
- **Starting material:** As-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi high-entropy alloy nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons characterized by high internal strain and defect densities typical of rapid deposition processes.
- **What is changed or studied:** A controlled post-deposition annealing treatment designed to partially relieve interfacial strain and reduce defect density.
- **Conditions:** Low applied magnetic fields (approximately 0.1–1 T) in magnetic-cooling cycles operating at frequencies where heat-transfer limitations are significant (e.g., 1–5 Hz), within the nanoscale dimensions of the films or ribbons.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Interfacial strain and defect density acting as localized modifiers of magnetic anisotropy.
- **Mechanism:** Annealing relaxes residual stresses and reduces defect concentrations at interfaces, thereby moderating localized magnetic anisotropy. This in turn alters the magnetic field-induced phase transitions that contribute to the magnetocaloric effect, leading to reduced hysteresis and a modified magnitude of the magnetocaloric response.
- **Predicted outcome:** Compared to the as-deposited baseline, annealed samples will exhibit a reduction in magnetic hysteresis and a shift in the magnetocaloric response (e.g., a more reversible temperature change per cycle). The magnitude of these changes is expected to be significant yet remains uncertain until quantified, due to the sensitivity of nanostructured heat flow and strain-relief dynamics.
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-deposited films or ribbons with a high level of internal interfacial strain and defect-induced anisotropy resulting in pronounced hysteresis and magnetocaloric behavior that differs from bulk responses.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis applies under low-field magnetic-cooling cycles in nanoscale thin film or nanoribbon geometries where heat-transfer rate limitations are non-negligible. It may not hold if geometric confinement effects or alternative thermal management strategies dominate the response.
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact quantitative impact of strain relaxation on hysteresis and the magnetocaloric effect in these nanoscale systems is uncertain, and the balance between microstructural changes and heat-transfer limitations requires empirical clarification.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare a set of as-deposited FeMnNiGeSi high-entropy alloy films/nanoribbons and subject half the samples to a controlled annealing procedure aimed at reducing strain and defects. Conduct low-applied-field magnetic-cooling cycle tests on both annealed and as-deposited samples while monitoring the magnetocaloric temperature change and hysteresis characteristics. Use transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or related techniques to assess changes in interfacial strain and defect density.
  - *Measurements:* Magnetic hysteresis loop area under low applied fields; Temperature change per magnetic field cycle (magnetocaloric effect); Microstructural characterization of strain and defect density (e.g., TEM, XRD line-broadening analyses)
  - *Falsified if:* If the annealed samples do not show a statistically significant reduction in hysteresis or adjustment in the magnetocaloric response relative to the as-deposited baseline—or if microstructural analyses fail to show a meaningful reduction in interfacial strain and defect density—then the proposed mechanism based on strain-mediated modification is not supported.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** In as-deposited Fe25Ni21Mn24Si13Ge17 low-dimensional structures, magnetocaloric response and hysteresis would be governed primarily by short-range atomic order, internal-strain-controlled domain pinning, and geometry-dependent magnetic coupling rather than by bulk-like crystallinity. Pre-crystallization annealing should relax pinning barriers and increase the effective ferromagnetic fraction, improving low-field reversibility and magnetic entropy change.
- **Starting material:** Rare-earth-free Fe25Ni21Mn24Si13Ge17 would be prepared as substrate-supported nanoscale thin films and lithographically patterned nanoribbon arrays. The as-deposited material would be amorphous or extremely fine and disordered, with cubic-type short-range order, no long-range crystalline order or secondary phases, and a smooth surface.
- **What is changed or studied:** The primary controlled intervention would be annealing a 1 µm-spaced nanoribbon array at approximately 700 K in inert gas. Complementary comparisons would apply 0–2% longitudinal elastic strain to continuous films and increase ribbon spacing from 1 to 2 µm.
- **Conditions:** Magnetization would be examined from about 2–300 K in fields parallel and perpendicular to the ribbon axis. Magnetocaloric isotherms would span roughly 5–200 K at fields up to 50 kOe. Magnetic response would be compared before and after annealing, strain, or spacing changes.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Annealing should preserve the amorphous matrix and cubic short-range order while inducing local atomic reordering, structural relaxation, and relief of fabrication strain without recrystallization, phase decomposition, grain growth, or substantial roughening. Strain should create residual magnetoelastic anisotropy, while 2 µm spacing should suppress inter-ribbon coupling.
- **Mechanism:** Relaxation and short-range reordering should lower local domain-pinning barriers, strengthen collective exchange and ferromagnetic alignment, and enlarge the effective ferromagnetic volume fraction. Longitudinal strain should stabilize magnetic order above 100 K through magnetoelastic coupling without shifting cluster blocking. Wider spacing should remove coupling-related anisotropy, whereas edge spin frustration and pinning should continue to suppress low-temperature ribbon magnetization relative to films.
- **Predicted outcome:** Annealing should raise 5 K magnetization from about 50 to 75 emu/g and increase the peak conventional −ΔSM near 140 K from approximately 1.25 to 1.35 J kg−1 K−1 at 50 kOe, while increasing low-field susceptibility and making directional loops more convergent. Strain should enhance magnetization above 100 K but leave the roughly 70 K blocking peak and in-plane isotropy essentially unchanged; 5 K magnetization could decrease slightly, from about 60 to 55 emu/g. At 2 µm spacing, parallel and perpendicular low-temperature loops should overlap.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The baseline would be otherwise identical as-deposited, unannealed, and unstrained films or 1 µm-spaced nanoribbons.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis would apply below crystallization, around 700 K rather than near 1000 K, and for elastic strain no greater than 2%. A broad conventional response should occur from about 40–200 K, with an inverse contribution below 20 K. Cooling-cycle frequency would remain limited by device and substrate heat-transfer rates, and entropy change should remain modest relative to ordered bulk alloys.
- **Main uncertainty:** The extent to which annealing-induced enhancement arises from atomic reordering rather than strain relief may remain difficult to separate.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare matched as-deposited and 700 K-annealed 1 µm-spaced nanoribbon arrays while verifying retention of the noncrystalline state.
  - *Measurements:* Parallel and perpendicular low-field and full M(H) loops; ZFC/FC M(T) curves; Temperature-resolved −ΔSM from magnetization isotherms; TEM/SAED, surface morphology, and temperature-dependent resistivity
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if annealing preserved the amorphous structure yet failed to increase magnetization or −ΔSM, or if any enhancement required detectable crystallization or phase formation.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The magnetocaloric response and hysteresis in as-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi-type high-entropy alloys in the form of nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons are governed by the interplay between the alloy's inherent magnetic anisotropy, the dimensional constraints imposed by the nanostructure, and the heat transfer rate limitations.
- **Starting material:** As-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi-type high-entropy alloys in the form of nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons
- **What is changed or studied:** Application of a controlled, low-applied-field magnetic-cooling cycle with varying cycle frequencies
- **Conditions:** Low-dimensional state, genuinely nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons
- **Structure through which it acts:** Magnetic anisotropy, dimensional constraints
- **Mechanism:** Interplay between magnetic anisotropy, dimensional constraints, and heat transfer rate limitations
- **Predicted outcome:** Reduced magnetocaloric response and increased hysteresis compared to bulk forms, with cycle frequency limited by heat transfer rate
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi-type high-entropy alloys in bulk form
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Low-applied-field magnetic-cooling cycles, genuinely low-dimensional state
- **Main uncertainty:** Insufficient evidence to fully explain the governing mechanisms, potential interactions between magnetic anisotropy, dimensional constraints, and heat transfer rate limitations
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled, low-applied-field magnetic-cooling cycles with varying cycle frequencies on as-deposited, non-equiatomic FeMnNiGeSi-type high-entropy alloys in the form of nanoscale thin films or lithographically patterned nanoribbons
  - *Measurements:* Magnetocaloric response, hysteresis, cycle frequency
  - *Falsified if:* If the magnetocaloric response and hysteresis are not significantly reduced compared to bulk forms, or if the cycle frequency is not limited by heat transfer rate, then the hypothesis is falsified
