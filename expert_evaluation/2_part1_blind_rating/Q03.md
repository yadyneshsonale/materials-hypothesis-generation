## The research question

> Starting from a eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies, what single intervention could overcome limited room-temperature tensile ductility and brittleness while retaining or increasing strength? The hypothesis should address the tendency for dislocation pile-ups near internal boundaries to produce local stress concentrations, microcracking, and catastrophic damage during straining. Specify the intervention, its mechanism, the predicted strength and ductility relative to the untreated starting material, the applicable testing and operating conditions, supporting prior evidence, and a falsification experiment.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Applying a single high-pressure, high-temperature treatment to homogenized Al0.7CoCrFeNi will replace its crack-prone lamellar architecture with a coherent hierarchical pattern, thereby approximately doubling both room-temperature tensile strength and ductility rather than trading one property for the other.
- **Starting material:** The study will start from homogenized eutectic Al0.7CoCrFeNi containing dual-phase FCC/B2 lamellar colonies. The softer FCC and harder B2 phases will initially be separated by largely incoherent internal interfaces, where dislocation pile-ups would promote local stress concentrations, interfacial microcracking, and brittle failure.
- **What is changed or studied:** The alloy will receive one high-pressure and high-temperature treatment in the solid state, followed by cooling and decompression.
- **Conditions:** Treatment will be conducted at approximately 6 GPa and 1470 K. Mechanical performance will be evaluated under room-temperature uniaxial tension, including interrupted testing to strains approaching 35%.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The treatment is expected to transform the dual-phase lamellae into a hierarchically patterned FCC/B2 microstructure with coherent interfaces while preserving the two-phase architecture.
- **Mechanism:** Coherent interfaces should permit preferential transfer of dislocations from the softer FCC phase into the harder B2 phase, reducing pile-ups and associated stress concentrations. The hierarchical pattern should also activate complementary dislocation slip, stacking faults, microbands, and deformation twins. Together, these processes would distribute strain more uniformly between phases, delay plastic instability, and replace catastrophic interfacial crack propagation with spatially distributed microcracking.
- **Predicted outcome:** The treated alloy should reach a fracture strength of about 1.1-1.2 GPa and a uniform tensile elongation of roughly 35-40% at room temperature. Both properties would be approximately twice those of the untreated alloy, while fracture would shift from elongated interfacial cracks toward isolated, distributed microcracks.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The comparator will be the untreated homogenized or conventionally cast eutectic alloy, which would retain strong-yet-brittle FCC/B2 lamellae, with strength and elongation roughly half the predicted treated values.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The synergy is expected only when pressure near 6 GPa at about 1470 K induces the coherent hierarchical pattern. Similar-temperature hot isostatic pressing or thermomechanical treatment at substantially lower pressure should not provide the same combined improvement.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty is whether pressure-temperature processing will reproducibly create sufficient interfacial coherence throughout the bulk alloy to enable phase-to-phase dislocation transfer.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare untreated and high-pressure-treated specimens in room-temperature tensile tests, including interruption near 35% strain and post-fracture microstructural examination.
  - *Measurements:* Fracture strength and uniform tensile elongation; FCC- and B2-phase geometrically necessary dislocation densities by EBSD; Fracture-surface morphology and crack distribution
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if treatment fails to produce approximately 1.1 GPa strength with about 35% elongation, or if phase-specific dislocation densities do not converge near 35% strain and elongated interfacial cracks remain dominant.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Introduction of nano-scale precipitates within the lamellar colonies of a eutectic high-entropy alloy can overcome limited room-temperature tensile ductility and brittleness while retaining or increasing strength.
- **Starting material:** Eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies
- **What is changed or studied:** Introduction of nano-scale precipitates
- **Conditions:** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile testing
- **Structure through which it acts:** Nano-scale precipitates
- **Mechanism:** The nano-scale precipitates can act as obstacles to dislocation movement, reducing the tendency for dislocation pile-ups near internal boundaries and thereby decreasing local stress concentrations, microcracking, and catastrophic damage during straining.
- **Predicted outcome:** Retained or increased strength and improved tensile ductility relative to the untreated starting material
- **Compared with (baseline):** Untreated eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile testing
- **Main uncertainty:** The effectiveness of the nano-scale precipitates in improving ductility and strength may depend on factors such as their size, distribution, and composition, which are not specified in this hypothesis.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Uniaxial tensile testing of the untreated and nano-precipitate-introduced alloys at room temperature
  - *Measurements:* Tensile strength; Tensile ductility; Microstructure characterization
  - *Falsified if:* If the introduction of nano-scale precipitates does not result in improved tensile ductility and retained or increased strength relative to the untreated alloy, the hypothesis is falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** A targeted post-solidification annealing treatment that selectively disrupts the coherent nature of the lamellar interphase boundaries can reduce dislocation pile-ups, thereby alleviating local stress concentrations and microcracking while retaining (or even modestly increasing) the alloy’s strength.
- **Starting material:** A eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical dual-phase lamellar colony microstructure in which relatively coherent lamellar interfaces promote dislocation pile-ups that lead to local stress concentrations and brittle failure at room temperature.
- **What is changed or studied:** Apply an optimized thermal annealing treatment (at a temperature and duration calibrated to induce controlled boundary decohesion or increased boundary incoherency without changing the overall phase composition) to modify the structure of the lamellar boundaries.
- **Conditions:** Room-temperature tensile testing under standard strain rates, ensuring that the treatment does not alter the high-temperature operational or processing conditions of the alloy.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The modified (less coherent) interlamellar boundaries that are more effective in absorbing or dispersing dislocations instead of allowing pile-ups.
- **Mechanism:** By inducing a controlled degree of atomic-scale disorder at the lamellar boundaries, the annealing treatment creates interfaces that are better able to absorb dislocations and mitigate pile-up formation. This reduces local stress concentrations that normally trigger microcrack nucleation, thereby improving ductility. Simultaneously, the retained dual-phase lamellar morphology preserves the intrinsic strength contributed by the eutectic structure.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to the untreated condition, the annealed alloy is predicted to display a noticeable increase in room-temperature tensile ductility (due to more diffuse dislocation dispersion) while maintaining, or slightly increasing, tensile strength (as the overall phase morphology is conserved).
- **Compared with (baseline):** The original eutectic high-entropy alloy exhibits limited ductility and brittleness stemming from dislocation accumulation at coherent internal boundaries.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The improvement in ductility and retention of strength is contingent on precisely controlling the annealing parameters; over-annealing might excessively relax the boundaries and decrease strength, while under-annealing would not yield the desired reduction in dislocation pile-ups.
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact annealing temperature and duration required to optimize the boundary incoherency without adversely affecting phase stability and strength enhancement remains to be determined, and there is some uncertainty regarding the sensitivity of the microstructure to slight changes in these parameters.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of alloy samples: one subjected to the proposed optimized annealing treatment and one left untreated. Conduct systematic room-temperature tensile tests while using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and fractography to assess dislocation distribution and crack nucleation sites.
  - *Measurements:* Stress-strain curves to quantify tensile strength and ductility; TEM images to evaluate the degree of boundary incoherency and dislocation distribution; Fractographic analysis to assess initiation and propagation of microcracks
  - *Falsified if:* If the annealed samples do not show a statistically significant reduction in dislocation pile-ups or if there is no measurable improvement in ductility (or a reduction in strength) compared to the untreated samples under identical testing conditions, then the hypothesis that the boundary modification reduces local stress concentrations without sacrificing strength is falsified.
