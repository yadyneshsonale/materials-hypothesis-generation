## The research question

> For a high-entropy alloy initially designed as a single-phase solid solution, what testable mechanistic hypothesis explains whether and why it loses toughness or undergoes brittle fracture under mechanical loading in demanding engineering service? Define a controlled comparison that would reveal the governing behavior, predict observations relative to the initial baseline, identify the applicable service and loading boundaries, cite supporting prior evidence, and state a result that would falsify the hypothesis.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Thermal aging of as-cast AlCoCrFeNi2.1 eutectic high-entropy alloy will produce a non-monotonic ductile-brittle-ductile response under mechanical loading. Maximum embrittlement near 600 °C will arise from coupled L12 chemical ordering within FCC domains and increased jaggedness of B2/FCC phase boundaries, rather than from conventional grain-boundary impurity segregation. Aging near 750 °C will suppress this ordering and generate ductile transition zones along phase boundaries, thereby restoring ductility.
- **Starting material:** The starting material will be as-cast AlCoCrFeNi2.1 eutectic high-entropy alloy containing FCC and B2 phases.
- **What is changed or studied:** The alloy will be thermally aged at intermediate temperatures, with controlled comparisons among the as-cast condition, an embrittlement condition near 600 °C, and a recovery condition near 750 °C.
- **Conditions:** The critical aging interval will be approximately 550–750 °C, where chemical-ordering kinetics and B2/FCC phase-boundary evolution should be maximized. Mechanical loading will then be used to compare deformation and fracture behavior across aging conditions.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Near 600 °C, FCC domains will develop enhanced L12 ordering approaching Ni3Al stoichiometry while B2/FCC interfaces will become more jagged. Near 750 °C, L12 ordering should diminish and more ductile transition zones should form along the phase boundaries.
- **Mechanism:** Enhanced L12 ordering will raise antiphase-boundary energy and elastic modulus, restrict dislocation cross-slip, and favor planar slip. Dislocation accumulation at sharp tips of jagged B2/FCC boundaries will concentrate stress and trigger plastic instability and crack initiation. Reduced ordering and ductile interfacial transition zones at higher aging temperatures will relax these constraints and permit ductility recovery.
- **Predicted outcome:** Ductility will remain comparatively stable below about 550 °C, fall to a pronounced minimum after aging near 600 °C, and recover substantially near or above 750 °C. The resulting ductile-brittle-ductile transition will track the combined prevalence of highly ordered L12 domains and jagged phase boundaries.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The mechanistic baseline will be classical thermally induced embrittlement in conventional alloys, in which impurity segregation promotes grain-boundary fracture. The proposed alloy response should instead be phase-boundary- and ordering-mediated without intergranular impurity segregation.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis will apply primarily to mechanically loaded service after aging: below roughly 550 °C no transition is expected, between about 550 and 750 °C a brittle window is expected, and above roughly 750 °C ductile recovery is expected.
- **Main uncertainty:** The relative contributions of elevated ordering strength and phase-boundary jaggedness may be difficult to separate because both should evolve within the same aging window.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare as-cast material with specimens aged near 600 and 750 °C under equivalent mechanical loading, while characterizing deformation and fracture sites by TKD or TEM.
  - *Measurements:* Dislocation pile-ups and planar slip within ordered FCC domains; Crack initiation at tips of jagged B2/FCC phase boundaries; L12 ordering and phase-boundary morphology; Presence or absence of intergranular impurity segregation; Relative ductility across aging temperatures
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if minimum ductility near 600 °C does not coincide with enhanced L12 ordering, dislocation pile-ups, and crack initiation at jagged B2/FCC boundary tips, or if fracture is instead governed by impurity-segregated grain boundaries.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Under sustained high mechanical loading, the single-phase high-entropy alloy undergoes localized compositional segregation that nucleates brittle, nano-scale precipitates at stress concentration sites. These precipitates then act as crack initiation points, leading to a reduction in toughness and promoting brittle fracture.
- **Starting material:** A homogeneous single-phase high-entropy alloy as initially engineered to display a uniform solid solution.
- **What is changed or studied:** Subject the alloy to controlled, high strain rate mechanical loading (e.g., cyclic fatigue or high stress monotonic loading) that mimics demanding engineering service conditions.
- **Conditions:** Mechanical tests performed at high strain rates and stress levels, possibly at temperatures relevant to service environments (e.g., elevated or sub-zero temperatures) to trigger deformation-driven segregation.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Formation of localized nano-scale brittle precipitates arising from stress-induced compositional segregation.
- **Mechanism:** High mechanical stresses promote local atomic mobility and segregation within an otherwise homogeneous phase, leading to the nucleation of brittle intermetallic or ordered phases at potential crack nucleation sites. These precipitates disrupt dislocation motion and concentrate stresses, thereby reducing the material’s toughness and leading to brittle fracture.
- **Predicted outcome:** Compared to the baseline homogeneous state, the loaded sample will exhibit microstructural evidence (via TEM/SEM) of precipitate formation at stress concentration regions along with reduced fracture toughness and an earlier onset of brittle fracture.
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-cast or annealed high-entropy alloy in its initial homogeneous, single-phase form, tested under low or non-degrading loading conditions and showing high toughness and ductility.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The phenomena are expected to manifest under demanding mechanical service conditions that involve high applied stresses and strain rates, potentially coupled with extreme service temperatures.
- **Main uncertainty:** While the hypothesis predicts stress-induced precipitation as the dominant mechanism, the precise conditions (e.g., critical stress level, strain rate threshold, or temperature window) necessary to trigger segregation and precipitation remain uncertain without further targeted experiments.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Perform a controlled series of mechanical tests on specimens of the high-entropy alloy under increasing levels of applied stress and strain rate, while maintaining a control group at low stress. Use microstructural characterization (TEM, SEM, and possibly atom probe tomography) pre- and post-loading to detect any signs of precipitation or segregation, and measure fracture toughness using standardized fracture mechanics tests.
  - *Measurements:* Fracture toughness (e.g., K_IC) measurements; Microstructural analysis of precipitate formation (TEM/SEM/atom probe); Mechanical stress-strain curves under cyclic or monotonic loading
  - *Falsified if:* If the mechanically loaded specimens do not exhibit any detectable localized brittle precipitate formation or compositional segregation—and their fracture toughness remains statistically similar to that of the baseline condition—then the hypothesis that stress-induced segregation leads to brittleness is falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The high-entropy alloy loses toughness or undergoes brittle fracture under mechanical loading due to the formation of nano-scale precipitates that act as stress concentrators, leading to crack initiation and propagation.
- **Starting material:** Single-phase solid solution high-entropy alloy
- **What is changed or studied:** Mechanical loading
- **Conditions:** Demanding engineering service conditions, such as high temperatures, high stresses, and corrosive environments
- **Structure through which it acts:** Nano-scale precipitates
- **Mechanism:** Stress concentration and crack initiation/propagation
- **Predicted outcome:** Loss of toughness or brittle fracture
- **Compared with (baseline):** Initial single-phase solid solution microstructure
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Service conditions exceeding the alloy's design limits, such as temperatures above 800°C or stresses above 1000 MPa
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact composition and processing history of the alloy, as well as the specific service conditions, may influence the formation and distribution of nano-scale precipitates
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled comparison of the high-entropy alloy's microstructure and mechanical properties before and after mechanical loading, using techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
  - *Measurements:* Microstructure characterization; Mechanical property testing (e.g., tensile testing, fracture toughness testing)
  - *Falsified if:* If no nano-scale precipitates are observed and the alloy's toughness is maintained after mechanical loading, the hypothesis is falsified
