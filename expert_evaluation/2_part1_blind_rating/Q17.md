## The research question

> For bulk HfNbTaTiZr, what testable hypothesis could an unspecified predictive approach make to overcome the limited accuracy of available analytical predictions and their usual restriction to perfect, defect-free crystals? Address the nearly 0–200 K regime and the 300–1200 K regime, for which measurements also exist on a specimen containing 1 wt.% oxygen; explain why the approach should improve accuracy, predict its advantage over existing analytical approaches, define its temperature and sample applicability limits, identify supporting prior evidence, and specify a held-out conductivity benchmark and result that would falsify the hypothesis.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** A combined Monte Carlo and non-equilibrium molecular-dynamics predictive approach would describe lattice heat transport in bcc HfNbTaTiZr more accurately than analytical treatments based on perfect, chemically random crystals because it would explicitly represent chemical short-range order (SRO), local lattice distortion, and connected transport pathways.
- **Starting material:** The starting material would be random, single-phase bcc equiatomic HfNbTaTiZr, with substantial local lattice distortion and no connected SRO clusters. An experimental specimen containing 1 wt.% oxygen would provide an additional comparison but would not be represented explicitly by the nominal alloy model.
- **What is changed or studied:** Monte Carlo sampling would generate states with varying SRO, including HfTi and TiZr clusters and B2-like local order. Non-equilibrium molecular dynamics would then predict lattice thermal conductivity for the random and SRO-containing states.
- **Conditions:** The principal quantitative predictions would apply from roughly 300 to 1200 K under a single-phase bcc constraint. The nearly 0–200 K data would be treated as a separate, stringent low-temperature benchmark because the classical atomistic transport approach would become less reliable toward 0 K.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The mediator would be the emergence and percolation of B2-like HfTi and TiZr SRO clusters, accompanied by an approximately 10–15% reduction in atomic displacements relative to the random state.
- **Mechanism:** SRO would reduce lattice distortion and therefore phonon scattering while creating comparatively high-conductivity percolation pathways. These effects would outweigh additional scattering from the ordered clusters themselves and would alter the high-frequency phonon density of states.
- **Predicted outcome:** Percolated SRO would increase lattice thermal conductivity by about 10–15%, centered near 12%, relative to the random-alloy baseline. Explicit treatment of local chemical structure would consequently yield better agreement with conductivity benchmarks than existing analytical approaches restricted to ideal crystals.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The baseline would be chemically random bcc HfNbTaTiZr with high lattice distortion, no SRO percolation, and conductivity estimated using analytical perfect-crystal assumptions.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The quantitative claim would be limited to single-phase bcc HfNbTaTiZr over approximately 300–1200 K. Extension to other bcc or fcc high-entropy alloys would require percolation of suitable ordered binary clusters; it would not imply the behavior typical of random fcc alloys, whose conductivity would generally decrease substantially with temperature. Oxygen-containing material would remain an external applicability test.
- **Main uncertainty:** The main uncertainty would be whether the simulated SRO topology and percolation threshold represent those in the nominal and 1 wt.% oxygen specimens.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Hold out lattice-conductivity data spanning nearly 0–200 K and 300–1200 K, including the 1 wt.% oxygen specimen, until predictions for random and SRO-containing states are fixed.
  - *Measurements:* Temperature-dependent lattice thermal conductivity; Relative conductivity change with SRO; Atomic-displacement change; High-frequency phonon density-of-states change
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if percolated SRO failed to produce an approximately 10–15% conductivity increase, or if the SRO-aware predictions did not outperform the analytical random perfect-crystal baseline on the held-out conductivity benchmark within the stated temperature and sample limits.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** A machine learning-based predictive approach can accurately model the thermal conductivity of bulk HfNbTaTiZr in the 0-200 K and 300-1200 K regimes, including the effects of oxygen impurities, by learning from a dataset of experimental measurements and first-principles calculations.
- **Starting material:** Bulk HfNbTaTiZr with and without 1 wt.% oxygen
- **What is changed or studied:** Introduction of oxygen impurities
- **Conditions:** Temperature range of 0-200 K and 300-1200 K
- **Structure through which it acts:** Crystal structure and defects
- **Mechanism:** Phonon-phonon interactions and electron-phonon scattering
- **Predicted outcome:** Improved accuracy in predicting thermal conductivity compared to existing analytical approaches
- **Compared with (baseline):** Existing analytical predictions for perfect, defect-free crystals
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range of 0-200 K and 300-1200 K, and sample composition with and without 1 wt.% oxygen
- **Main uncertainty:** Limited by the quality and quantity of training data, and the complexity of the underlying physical mechanisms
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Measure the thermal conductivity of bulk HfNbTaTiZr with and without 1 wt.% oxygen at temperatures between 0-200 K and 300-1200 K, and compare the results to the predictions made by the machine learning-based approach
  - *Measurements:* Thermal conductivity; Temperature; Sample composition
  - *Falsified if:* If the predicted thermal conductivity values deviate from the measured values by more than 10% in either the 0-200 K or 300-1200 K regime, the hypothesis is falsified

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** A predictive simulation method that explicitly incorporates defect and oxygen impurity effects (e.g., via microstructural or machine learning–enhanced potentials) will yield electrical conductivity predictions for bulk HfNbTaTiZr that are significantly closer to experimental measurements in both the low (0–200 K) and high (300–1200 K) temperature regimes than conventional analytical approaches based on perfect crystals.
- **Starting material:** Polycrystalline bulk HfNbTaTiZr with naturally present defect distributions and a controlled oxygen impurity level of approximately 1 wt.%.
- **What is changed or studied:** Apply a predictive approach that integrates defect-induced scattering and the effect of oxygen impurities into conductivity calculations. This may be realized by coupling atomistic simulations (e.g., ab initio or ML-informed interatomic potentials) calibrated against experimental microstructural and oxygen-content data.
- **Conditions:** Two temperature windows: 0–200 K (where quantum and defect scattering effects dominate) and 300–1200 K (where phonon scattering and oxygen-related defect scattering become more significant). Comparisons should be made on specimens with and without the 1 wt.% oxygen addition.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Defects and oxygen atoms that introduce local disorder and scattering centers in the alloy’s lattice.
- **Mechanism:** Inclusion of realistic defect and impurity profiles in the simulation adjusts the electron/phonon scattering rates. By accounting for these scattering processes, the model can predict reductions or modulations in conductivity relative to ideal, defect‐free assumptions, thereby capturing the temperature-dependent impact of impurities more accurately.
- **Predicted outcome:** The modified model will predict electrical conductivities that differ from those of perfect crystal analytical models—showing, for instance, a measurable decrease (or other systematic deviation) in conductivity due to defect scattering, and a closer alignment with available experimental benchmarks in both the near-0-200 K and 300-1200 K regimes. The advantage lies in its ability to bridge the gap between idealized predictions and real-material behavior affected by defects and impurities.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Existing analytical predictions based on perfect, defect-free crystals.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** This approach is applicable to bulk HfNbTaTiZr under typical processing conditions with up to ~1 wt.% oxygen. Its predictive accuracy beyond the temperature range 0–200 K and 300–1200 K, or for substantially different impurity levels and microstructures, may not be assured.
- **Main uncertainty:** While the inclusion of defect and impurity effects is expected to improve agreement with experiment, the precise magnitude of improvement is subject to uncertainties in defect parameterization and impurity interaction modeling. Experimental calibration will be necessary to quantify these effects.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare series of bulk HfNbTaTiZr samples with controlled oxygen concentrations (pure and 1 wt.% oxygen) and perform precision electrical conductivity measurements over the 0–200 K and 300–1200 K ranges. Simultaneously, compute conductivity using both the conventional perfect-crystal analytical model and the defect-inclusive predictive model. Compare model outputs to experimental data.
  - *Measurements:* Temperature-dependent electrical conductivity (using techniques such as four-point probe measurements or equivalent high-precision methods); Detailed microstructural and compositional analysis (e.g., electron microscopy, spectroscopy) to quantify defect distributions and oxygen content
  - *Falsified if:* If the defect-inclusive predictive approach does not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement (e.g., exceeding a 10% reduction in prediction error) over the conventional perfect-crystal predictions in at least one of the temperature regimes, the hypothesis would be considered falsified.
