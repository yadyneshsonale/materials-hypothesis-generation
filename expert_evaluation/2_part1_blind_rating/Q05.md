## The research question

> As-cast HfMoTaTiZr has high strength at room temperature and elevated temperature but a relatively high density of 10.21 g cm−3. What single intervention could reduce its density without sacrificing its mechanical properties at room temperature and at elevated temperatures, including approximately 1000 °C? State the proposed intervention and mechanism, predict directional changes relative to as-cast HfMoTaTiZr, define the applicable temperature and loading boundaries, identify supporting prior evidence, and propose an experiment and rejection criterion that would falsify the hypothesis.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Adding approximately 4.2 at.% Cr to as-cast HfMoTaTiZr would lower alloy density while simultaneously increasing room-temperature compressive yield strength and fracture strain. This combination would depend on Cr partitioning between a strongly distorted BCC solid solution and a small, interconnected Laves-phase network rather than forming discrete brittle particles.
- **Starting material:** The reference material will be an as-cast Hf-Mo-Ta-Ti-Zr ingot with a single-phase BCC structure, a density of approximately 10.2–10.4 g cm−3, and a room-temperature compressive fracture strain near 12%.
- **What is changed or studied:** Approximately 4.2 at.% Cr will be added by vacuum arc melting, corresponding to the nominal HfMoTaTiZrCr0.5 alloy.
- **Conditions:** The principal evaluation will use room-temperature compression. The Cr-modified alloy is expected to contain about 4 vol.% secondary phase within the BCC matrix.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Cr addition should produce a reticulated, interconnected complex Laves phase, Cr2(Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta), throughout the BCC matrix. It should also contract the BCC lattice parameter from approximately 3.39 Å to 3.33 Å and increase lattice distortion and dislocation density.
- **Mechanism:** Cr incorporation would strengthen the BCC matrix through solute-size mismatch, severe lattice distortion, and associated dislocation accumulation. Although the Laves phase would be intrinsically brittle, its low-volume-fraction reticulated morphology should hinder crack propagation and distribute deformation, compensating for brittleness and preserving or increasing plasticity.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to as-cast HfMoTaTiZr, density should decrease, yield strength should rise by roughly 10–15% to about 1.75 GPa, and fracture strain should rise by roughly 20% to about 14%. Vickers hardness should increase to approximately 535 HV. These improvements would therefore occur concurrently rather than through the usual strength–ductility trade-off.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The comparator will be HfMoTaTiZr prepared by the same vacuum arc melting route, consistent with the Juan et al. alloy baseline.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The prediction applies to compressive loading at room temperature and requires an approximately 4 vol.% interconnected Laves network plus measurable BCC lattice contraction. Discrete Laves particles or excessive brittle-phase formation would fall outside the hypothesis. Mechanical-property retention at elevated temperatures, including approximately 1000 °C, will not be claimed without corresponding high-temperature tests.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty is whether small solidification differences would convert the beneficial reticulated Laves network into disconnected particles, changing crack propagation and ductility.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare matched as-cast HfMoTaTiZr and Cr-modified alloys, determine density, identify BCC and Laves phases, quantify Laves-phase volume fraction and connectivity, compare lattice parameters, and conduct room-temperature compression and hardness testing.
  - *Measurements:* Density; Laves-phase volume fraction and reticulated morphology; BCC lattice parameter; Compressive yield strength and fracture strain; Vickers hardness
  - *Falsified if:* Reject the hypothesis if Cr does not reduce density, if an approximately 4 vol.% reticulated Laves network and BCC lattice contraction are present but yield strength and fracture strain do not both increase relative to the matched baseline, or if improved strength is accompanied by reduced fracture strain.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Substituting Ta with Nb in the as‐cast HfMoTaTiZr alloy (i.e. forming HfMoNbTiZr) will reduce the alloy’s density while maintaining its high-strength characteristics at room temperature and around 1000 °C.
- **Starting material:** as‐cast HfMoTaTiZr high-entropy alloy with a single-phase microstructure
- **What is changed or studied:** Replace Ta with an equivalent atomic fraction of Nb so that the alloy becomes HfMoNbTiZr.
- **Conditions:** Mechanical loading at room temperature and elevated temperatures up to approximately 1000 °C.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Retention of a single-phase solid-solution (likely BCC) microstructure which governs high-temperature strength and room-temperature ductility.
- **Mechanism:** By substituting Ta (a heavier refractory element with high density) with Nb (which has a lower atomic mass yet possesses similar high-temperature phase stability and contributes to solid-solution strengthening), the overall density is reduced without a significant sacrifice in intrinsic strengthening mechanisms.
- **Predicted outcome:** A noticeable reduction in density (potentially on the order of a 5–10% decrease, given Nb’s lower density compared to Ta) with mechanical properties (e.g., yield strength and high-temperature strength) staying comparable to the original alloy.
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-cast HfMoTaTiZr alloy exhibiting high strength at room temperature and around 1000 °C with a density of 10.21 g/cm³.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis applies within the temperature range from room temperature up to ~1000 °C under conventional mechanical loading conditions (e.g., tensile or compressive stress levels typical of high-strength applications). Effects beyond this range or under unusual service conditions remain uncertain.
- **Main uncertainty:** It remains uncertain if the solid-solution strengthening mechanisms provided by Nb fully emulate those of Ta across all temperature regimes, and potential differences in oxidation or creep behavior at 1000 °C need further verification.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Synthesize as-cast HfMoNbTiZr alloy under identical processing conditions as the HfMoTaTiZr alloy. Measure and compare the density, microstructure (to confirm retention of a single-phase solid solution), and mechanical properties (e.g., yield strength, tensile strength, and ductility) at room temperature and at approximately 1000 °C.
  - *Measurements:* Density measurement using Archimedes’ method; X-ray diffraction for phase analysis and microstructure confirmation; Tensile tests at room temperature and at 1000 °C to assess yield and ultimate strengths; Creep or high-temperature deformation tests if applicable
  - *Falsified if:* If the HfMoNbTiZr alloy does not exhibit a statistically significant reduction in density (e.g., less than a 5% reduction) or if its mechanical properties (e.g., yield strength or high-temperature strength) fall more than 5–10% below those of the as-cast HfMoTaTiZr alloy, then the hypothesis that substituting Ta with Nb achieves the desired balance is falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** Introduction of pores via selective laser sintering can reduce the density of HfMoTaTiZr without sacrificing its mechanical properties at room temperature and elevated temperatures.
- **Starting material:** As-cast HfMoTaTiZr
- **What is changed or studied:** Introduction of pores via selective laser sintering
- **Conditions:** Room temperature and elevated temperatures up to 1000 °C
- **Structure through which it acts:** Pore structure and distribution
- **Mechanism:** The introduction of pores can reduce the overall density of the material while maintaining its strength due to the retention of a continuous, load-bearing network of HfMoTaTiZr. The pores may also act as sites for stress concentration relief, potentially improving toughness.
- **Predicted outcome:** Reduced density with maintained or improved mechanical properties at room and elevated temperatures.
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-cast HfMoTaTiZr with a density of 10.21 g cm−3
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range: room temperature to 1000 °C; Loading conditions: tensile, compressive, and impact loading
- **Main uncertainty:** The optimal pore size, distribution, and volume fraction to achieve the desired density reduction without compromising mechanical properties are uncertain and require experimental determination.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare HfMoTaTiZr samples with varying pore volumes introduced via selective laser sintering. Measure density and perform mechanical testing (tensile, compressive, and impact) at room temperature and 1000 °C.
  - *Measurements:* Density; Tensile strength; Compressive strength; Impact toughness
  - *Falsified if:* If the introduction of pores results in a significant reduction in mechanical properties (strength or toughness) at either room temperature or 1000 °C, relative to as-cast HfMoTaTiZr, the hypothesis is falsified.
