## The research question

> Equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state has favorable plasticity and machinability but relatively low hardness and strength, while its deformation behavior remains poorly understood. Under mechanical-property and deformation testing, what testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain what governs this low hardness and strength and why? Define a controlled comparison, predict the observations relative to the starting state, state the applicable composition, structural-state, and loading boundaries, connect the hypothesis to the reported property combination, and specify a falsification test.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** The relatively low hardness and strength of nominally equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a coarse, simple solid-solution or cast state would be governed primarily by insufficient grain-boundary and dislocation strengthening, compounded by porosity and microstructural heterogeneity. Producing a dense, strained nanocrystalline FCC/BCC solid solution should therefore substantially increase hardness and compressive strength, although at the cost of ductility.
- **Starting material:** The starting material would comprise high-purity elemental Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, and Ti powders in a nominally equimolar composition.
- **What is changed or studied:** The powders would undergo high-energy ball milling under argon for approximately 1–6 hours, followed by spark-plasma sintering under vacuum at about 900–1000 °C. Comparisons would be made across milling duration and sintering temperature and against the cast alloy state.
- **Conditions:** Milling would be conducted in an inert atmosphere to promote mechanical alloying, lattice strain, and crystallite refinement. Vacuum sintering within the stated temperature window would promote rapid diffusion and densification while limiting porosity and excessive grain growth.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Longer milling should generate a highly strained, non-equilibrium nanocrystalline solid solution with crystallites below roughly 50 nm. Subsequent sintering near 1000 °C should stabilize a dense mixed structure containing approximately 80–85% FCC and 15–20% BCC phase.
- **Mechanism:** Strengthening would arise synergistically from Hall–Petch grain-boundary strengthening, milling-induced dislocation accumulation, and substitutional solid-solution strengthening. Titanium’s comparatively large atomic radius and melting-point-related thermodynamic influence should help stabilize the FCC/BCC phase mixture. The resulting high grain-boundary fraction should favor intergranular fracture and limit ductility.
- **Predicted outcome:** Near the upper end of the sintering window, the alloy should reach a density above about 6.5 g/cm³, Vickers hardness of roughly 650–700 HV, compressive strength near 1.9 GPa, and yield stress near 1.2 GPa. Fracture should be predominantly intergranular, consistent with high strength but restricted plasticity.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Cast CoCrFeNiTi would be expected to retain greater porosity and microstructural heterogeneity and consequently show substantially lower hardness and strength than the milled-and-sintered condition.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis would apply to equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi processed into a nanocrystalline mixed FCC/BCC state under compression or indentation. Benefits should saturate near 1000 °C; excessive thermal exposure or milling beyond the useful window could cause grain coarsening or phase decomposition.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty is whether phase balance or nanocrystalline grain size would be the dominant contributor to strengthening, because both should change during processing.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare controlled CoCrFeNiTi conditions spanning 1–6 hours of milling and 900–1000 °C sintering, together with a cast baseline.
  - *Measurements:* Vickers hardness; X-ray-diffraction-derived FCC/BCC fractions and crystallite size; Compressive yield and maximum strength; Density and fracture mode
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if formation of a dense, sub-50-nm, approximately 80/20 FCC/BCC structure failed to produce a large hardness and strength increase over the cast baseline, or if strength did not track grain refinement and accumulated lattice strain.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The low hardness and strength of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state are governed by the ease of dislocation glide and climb due to the lack of significant lattice distortions and the presence of a large number of slip systems.
- **Starting material:** Equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state
- **What is changed or studied:** Mechanical-property and deformation testing
- **Conditions:** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile loading
- **Structure through which it acts:** Dislocation glide and climb
- **Mechanism:** Lack of significant lattice distortions and presence of a large number of slip systems
- **Predicted outcome:** Low hardness and strength compared to other high-entropy alloys with more complex solid-solution states or precipitated phases
- **Compared with (baseline):** Other high-entropy alloys with more complex solid-solution states or precipitated phases
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Composition: equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi; Structural state: simple solid-solution state; Loading: uniaxial tensile loading
- **Main uncertainty:** Deformation behavior at high temperatures or under multiaxial loading is not well understood
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare the mechanical properties and deformation behavior of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state to those of a high-entropy alloy with a more complex solid-solution state or precipitated phases
  - *Measurements:* Hardness; Strength; Dislocation density; Lattice distortion
  - *Falsified if:* If the hardness and strength of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi are found to be significantly higher than those of the comparison alloy, the hypothesis is falsified

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The relatively low hardness and strength of equimolar (or nearly equimolar) CoCrFeNiTi arises because its homogeneous, single‐phase solid solution promotes rapid dynamic recovery through facile dislocation cross-slip due to a moderately low stacking‐fault energy. This dynamic recovery prevents significant dislocation accumulation during deformation, thus limiting work hardening even though the alloy’s uniform nature favors plasticity and machinability.
- **Starting material:** Equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a fully annealed, single-phase solid-solution state.
- **What is changed or studied:** Perform mechanical deformation tests (e.g., tensile or compression tests) under controlled temperature and strain-rate conditions to modulate dynamic recovery effects.
- **Conditions:** Comparison between room-temperature tests (where dynamic recovery is active) and low-temperature tests (where reduced atomic mobility is expected to suppress recovery). The strain levels should be moderate (up to about 10%-15% strain) to monitor initial work hardening behavior.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Dislocation density and stacking-fault configurations (indicative of cross-slip/dynamic recovery processes).
- **Mechanism:** At room temperature, the alloy’s low stacking-fault energy and uniform atomic environment facilitate quick dislocation cross-slip and recovery, preventing the accumulation of dislocations necessary for significant hardening. Under conditions that suppress recovery (e.g., lower temperatures), dislocation accumulation should increase, leading to an augmented hardness and strength response.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to the starting annealed state, room-temperature deformation will show lower dislocation density buildup and a low strain-hardening rate, while low-temperature deformation is predicted to exhibit a higher dislocation density, increased work hardening, and consequently higher measured hardness and strength.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Room temperature deformation of the annealed equimolar CoCrFeNiTi, which currently shows good plasticity but low hardness/strength due to active dynamic recovery.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis applies to equimolar or near-equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in its single-phase solid-solution structure deformed at moderate strains (&lt;15%) and within temperature regimes where atomic mobility allows or suppresses dynamic recovery (room temperature vs. cryogenic temperatures).
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact quantitative influence of stacking-fault energy and the relative contributions of cross-slip versus other recovery processes are not fully established and require further microstructural investigation.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare equimolar CoCrFeNiTi samples in the annealed, single-phase state. Conduct deformation experiments (e.g., tensile or compression tests) at both room temperature and a significantly lower temperature (e.g., cryogenic conditions). Use in-situ or post-deformation transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to measure dislocation density and look for evidence of dynamic recovery (e.g., reduced dislocation pile-ups, prevalent cross-slip features). Measure mechanical responses (e.g., stress-strain curves, hardness) in each condition.
  - *Measurements:* Flow stress and strain-hardening rate from stress-strain curves; Microhardness values pre- and post-deformation; Dislocation density and stacking fault observations via TEM
  - *Falsified if:* If suppression of dynamic recovery at low temperatures does not lead to a significant increase in dislocation density, work hardening, and hardness/strength relative to room-temperature tests – or if room temperature tests already reveal high dislocation accumulation contrary to the predicted recovery – then the hypothesis that dynamic recovery governs the low hardness and strength would be falsified.
