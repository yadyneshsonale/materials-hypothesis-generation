# Q01

## The research question

> Equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state has favorable plasticity and machinability but relatively low hardness and strength, while its deformation behavior remains poorly understood. Under mechanical-property and deformation testing, what testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain what governs this low hardness and strength and why? Define a controlled comparison, predict the observations relative to the starting state, state the applicable composition, structural-state, and loading boundaries, connect the hypothesis to the reported property combination, and specify a falsification test.

---

### Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** The relatively low hardness and strength of nominally equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a coarse, simple solid-solution or cast state would be governed primarily by insufficient grain-boundary and dislocation strengthening, compounded by porosity and microstructural heterogeneity. Producing a dense, strained nanocrystalline FCC/BCC solid solution should therefore substantially increase hardness and compressive strength, although at the cost of ductility.
- **Starting material:** The starting material would comprise high-purity elemental Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, and Ti powders in a nominally equimolar composition.
- **What is changed or studied:** The powders would undergo high-energy ball milling under argon for approximately 1–6 hours, followed by spark-plasma sintering under vacuum at about 900–1000 °C. Comparisons would be made across milling duration and sintering temperature and against the cast alloy state.
- **Conditions:** Milling would be conducted in an inert atmosphere to promote mechanical alloying, lattice strain, and crystallite refinement. Vacuum sintering within the stated temperature window would promote rapid diffusion and densification while limiting porosity and excessive grain growth.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Longer milling should generate a highly strained, non-equilibrium nanocrystalline solid solution with crystallites below roughly 50 nm. Subsequent sintering near 1000 °C should stabilize a dense mixed structure containing approximately 80–85% FCC and 15–20% BCC phase.
- **Mechanism:** Strengthening would arise synergistically from Hall–Petch grain-boundary strengthening, milling-induced dislocation accumulation, and substitutional solid-solution strengthening. Titanium’s comparatively large atomic radius and melting-point-related thermodynamic influence should help stabilize the FCC/BCC phase mixture. The resulting high grain-boundary fraction should favor intergranular fracture and limit ductility.
- **Predicted outcome:** Near the upper end of the sintering window, the alloy should reach a density above about 6.5 g/cm³, Vickers hardness of roughly 650–700 HV, compressive strength near 1.9 GPa, and yield stress near 1.2 GPa. Fracture should be predominantly intergranular, consistent with high strength but restricted plasticity.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Cast CoCrFeNiTi would be expected to retain greater porosity and microstructural heterogeneity and consequently show substantially lower hardness and strength than the milled-and-sintered condition.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis would apply to equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi processed into a nanocrystalline mixed FCC/BCC state under compression or indentation. Benefits should saturate near 1000 °C; excessive thermal exposure or milling beyond the useful window could cause grain coarsening or phase decomposition.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty is whether phase balance or nanocrystalline grain size would be the dominant contributor to strengthening, because both should change during processing.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare controlled CoCrFeNiTi conditions spanning 1–6 hours of milling and 900–1000 °C sintering, together with a cast baseline.
  - *Measurements:* Vickers hardness; X-ray-diffraction-derived FCC/BCC fractions and crystallite size; Compressive yield and maximum strength; Density and fracture mode
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if formation of a dense, sub-50-nm, approximately 80/20 FCC/BCC structure failed to produce a large hardness and strength increase over the cast baseline, or if strength did not track grain refinement and accumulated lattice strain.

---

### Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The low hardness and strength of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state are governed by the ease of dislocation glide and climb due to the lack of significant lattice distortions and the presence of a large number of slip systems.
- **Starting material:** Equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state
- **What is changed or studied:** Mechanical-property and deformation testing
- **Conditions:** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile loading
- **Structure through which it acts:** Dislocation glide and climb
- **Mechanism:** Lack of significant lattice distortions and presence of a large number of slip systems
- **Predicted outcome:** Low hardness and strength compared to other high-entropy alloys with more complex solid-solution states or precipitated phases
- **Compared with (baseline):** Other high-entropy alloys with more complex solid-solution states or precipitated phases
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Composition: equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi; Structural state: simple solid-solution state; Loading: uniaxial tensile loading
- **Main uncertainty:** Deformation behavior at high temperatures or under multiaxial loading is not well understood
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare the mechanical properties and deformation behavior of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a simple solid-solution state to those of a high-entropy alloy with a more complex solid-solution state or precipitated phases
  - *Measurements:* Hardness; Strength; Dislocation density; Lattice distortion
  - *Falsified if:* If the hardness and strength of equimolar or nearly equimolar CoCrFeNiTi are found to be significantly higher than those of the comparison alloy, the hypothesis is falsified

---

### Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The relatively low hardness and strength of equimolar (or nearly equimolar) CoCrFeNiTi arises because its homogeneous, single‐phase solid solution promotes rapid dynamic recovery through facile dislocation cross-slip due to a moderately low stacking‐fault energy. This dynamic recovery prevents significant dislocation accumulation during deformation, thus limiting work hardening even though the alloy’s uniform nature favors plasticity and machinability.
- **Starting material:** Equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in a fully annealed, single-phase solid-solution state.
- **What is changed or studied:** Perform mechanical deformation tests (e.g., tensile or compression tests) under controlled temperature and strain-rate conditions to modulate dynamic recovery effects.
- **Conditions:** Comparison between room-temperature tests (where dynamic recovery is active) and low-temperature tests (where reduced atomic mobility is expected to suppress recovery). The strain levels should be moderate (up to about 10%-15% strain) to monitor initial work hardening behavior.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Dislocation density and stacking-fault configurations (indicative of cross-slip/dynamic recovery processes).
- **Mechanism:** At room temperature, the alloy’s low stacking-fault energy and uniform atomic environment facilitate quick dislocation cross-slip and recovery, preventing the accumulation of dislocations necessary for significant hardening. Under conditions that suppress recovery (e.g., lower temperatures), dislocation accumulation should increase, leading to an augmented hardness and strength response.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to the starting annealed state, room-temperature deformation will show lower dislocation density buildup and a low strain-hardening rate, while low-temperature deformation is predicted to exhibit a higher dislocation density, increased work hardening, and consequently higher measured hardness and strength.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Room temperature deformation of the annealed equimolar CoCrFeNiTi, which currently shows good plasticity but low hardness/strength due to active dynamic recovery.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis applies to equimolar or near-equimolar CoCrFeNiTi in its single-phase solid-solution structure deformed at moderate strains (&lt;15%) and within temperature regimes where atomic mobility allows or suppresses dynamic recovery (room temperature vs. cryogenic temperatures).
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact quantitative influence of stacking-fault energy and the relative contributions of cross-slip versus other recovery processes are not fully established and require further microstructural investigation.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare equimolar CoCrFeNiTi samples in the annealed, single-phase state. Conduct deformation experiments (e.g., tensile or compression tests) at both room temperature and a significantly lower temperature (e.g., cryogenic conditions). Use in-situ or post-deformation transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to measure dislocation density and look for evidence of dynamic recovery (e.g., reduced dislocation pile-ups, prevalent cross-slip features). Measure mechanical responses (e.g., stress-strain curves, hardness) in each condition.
  - *Measurements:* Flow stress and strain-hardening rate from stress-strain curves; Microhardness values pre- and post-deformation; Dislocation density and stacking fault observations via TEM
  - *Falsified if:* If suppression of dynamic recovery at low temperatures does not lead to a significant increase in dislocation density, work hardening, and hardness/strength relative to room-temperature tests – or if room temperature tests already reveal high dislocation accumulation contrary to the predicted recovery – then the hypothesis that dynamic recovery governs the low hardness and strength would be falsified.

# Q02

## The research question

> For additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 high-entropy alloy with an fcc matrix, what testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain what governs degradation during elevated-temperature exposure to oxidizing or reducing gases and why catastrophic failure may occur? Specify a controlled change that would reveal the governing behavior, predicted observations relative to the starting-state baseline, applicable exposure boundaries, supporting prior evidence, and a clear falsification criterion.

---

### Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** In additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3, elevated-temperature exposure in oxidizing versus reducing gas environments alters the formation and stability of a protective surface oxide scale. A robust, continuous Al/Cr-rich oxide that forms under moderately oxidizing conditions slows degradation, while reducing or excessively oxidative conditions disrupt its formation or adherence. The resulting compromised scale leads to internal oxidation, stress concentration, and ultimately catastrophic failure via rapid crack propagation.
- **Starting material:** As-produced, fcc-matrix high-entropy alloy with a fairly homogeneous elemental distribution and residual stresses from the additive manufacturing process.
- **What is changed or studied:** Expose specimens to carefully controlled atmospheres where oxygen partial pressure is systematically varied (e.g., switching from a moderately oxidizing gas to a reducing gas or vice versa) at elevated temperatures.
- **Conditions:** Elevated temperatures (e.g., 700–900 °C) with controlled gas environments ranging from mildly oxidizing (e.g., air or diluted O2) to reducing (e.g., H2-containing atmosphere) conditions.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The integrity and composition of the surface oxide layer (specifically, formation and adhesion of an AlCr-rich oxide scale) that mediates the alloy’s resistance to further degradation.
- **Mechanism:** Under moderately oxidizing conditions, a coherent, adherent AlCr oxide scale develops that protects the underlying fcc matrix. Under reducing or excessively oxidative conditions, this scale becomes discontinuous or poorly adherent, permitting internal oxidation and element depletion, which increases intergranular stresses and facilitates crack initiation and propagation.
- **Predicted outcome:** Specimens exposed to the optimal oxidizing conditions will develop a protective oxide scale and show slower degradation (as measured by weight gain/loss, microstructural integrity, and mechanical property retention), whereas specimens in reducing or overly oxidative conditions will show evidence of compromised oxide layers, accelerated internal oxidation, embrittlement, and ultimately catastrophic failure (e.g., rapid drop in mechanical strength and crack formation).
- **Compared with (baseline):** The as-manufactured alloy exhibits a homogeneous microstructure with residual stresses but without significant oxide scale formation or catastrophic failure in the absence of extreme gas environments.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The predicted behavior is expected at temperatures above ~700 °C and is sensitive to gas-phase oxygen activity; deviations outside these exposure boundaries (e.g., significantly lower temperatures or near-inert atmospheres) may not trigger the same degradation mechanisms.
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact temperature thresholds and the quantitative influence of slight variations in oxygen partial pressure on oxide scale formation and failure kinetics remain uncertain without targeted empirical calibration.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare a series of additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 samples and expose them to controlled furnaces set at 700–900 °C under varying gas compositions: one set in a moderately oxidizing atmosphere (e.g., air or diluted O2) and another set in a reducing atmosphere (e.g., H2-containing gas). Monitor the evolution of oxide scale formation, mechanical properties, and crack propagation over time.
  - *Measurements:* Surface oxide morphology and composition via SEM/EDS and XRD analysis.; Weight gain/loss measurements to infer oxidation rates.; Mechanical property assessments (e.g., tensile strength, fracture toughness) before and after exposure.; In situ or post-mortem analysis of crack initiation and propagation using microscopy.
  - *Falsified if:* If the evolution of the surface oxide scale, internal oxidation patterns, and the associated mechanical degradation do not differ as predicted between the oxidizing and reducing conditions, or if no correlation between scale integrity and catastrophic failure is observed, then the hypothesis is falsified.

---

### Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Precipitation-induced Cr heterogeneity will govern degradation of additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 during elevated-temperature oxidation. Aging will create Cr-depleted precipitates and interfaces that delay establishment of a continuous Cr2O3 barrier, increasing oxygen ingress and mass gain; above about 900°C, defect-assisted penetration of that barrier could promote extensive internal oxidation and potentially catastrophic scale failure.
- **Starting material:** The starting alloy will be selective-laser-melted Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 from prealloyed powder, with greater than 97% density and a single-fcc cellular microstructure containing dislocation-rich cell walls.
- **What is changed or studied:** The controlled change will be direct aging near 750°C. This treatment should generate roughly 25–30 vol% dual nanoprecipitates: homogeneous Ni/Ti-enriched L12 particles and larger Al/Ti-enriched, Cr/Fe-depleted L21 particles concentrated near cell walls. Oxidation of this state will be compared with the unaged state.
- **Conditions:** Both states will undergo isothermal elevated-temperature exposure over 600–1200°C for up to about 100 h. The gas composition will remain unspecified, so conclusions will apply to the oxidation environment used rather than to all oxidizing or reducing gases.
- **Structure through which it acts:** A duplex-to-trilayer scale should form, comprising outer elemental and mixed or spinel oxides, a continuous Cr-rich layer dominated by Cr2O3, and a discontinuous inner Al/Ti-rich region. Cr-depleted precipitates and matrix–precipitate interfaces should delay protective-layer continuity and provide short-circuit oxygen paths.
- **Mechanism:** Initial oxidation should involve loose or discontinuous oxides of multiple constituents. Preferential outward Cr diffusion should then establish dense Cr2O3 that restricts inward oxygen and outward metal-ion transport. Cr should dominate continuous protection because its mobility is more favorable, whereas Al3+ and Ti4+ should diffuse more slowly and their low concentrations should prevent continuous Al/Ti-rich layers. At higher temperature, oxygen should traverse scale defects, microcracks, or grain-boundary channels and thicken the inner oxide.
- **Predicted outcome:** The aged alloy should gain consistently more mass than the as-printed baseline, with the difference increasing with temperature, but without a large overall oxidation penalty. Power-law exponents should be roughly 0.3–0.6 at 600–900°C, with aged material approaching parabolic behavior near 900°C. Both states should show nearly parabolic thickness growth above 900°C and similar apparent activation energies near 150 kJ/mol.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The baseline will be the as-printed single-fcc cellular alloy without L12/L21 precipitates. Its more uniform Cr distribution should permit earlier formation of a protective Cr-rich scale.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Cr2O3-mediated protection should be most effective through about 900°C. Above this threshold, rapid oxygen transport and inner Al/Ti-rich oxidation should become increasingly important. The hypothesis will be limited to isothermal exposures of 600–1200°C and no more than about 100 h.
- **Main uncertainty:** The unspecified gas composition could alter oxide stability and transport, particularly under reducing conditions.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Expose as-printed and 750°C-aged specimens isothermally across 600–1200°C while tracking oxidation kinetics and scale development.
  - *Measurements:* Time-resolved mass gain with power-law exponent and rate-constant fitting; Cross-sectional oxide thickness and Arrhenius activation energy; Cr, Al, Ti, and oxygen distributions across the scale; Identification of Cr-rich, Al/Ti-rich, and mixed oxides
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if aging does not increase mass gain or delay continuous Cr2O3 formation, or if degradation differences occur without corresponding Cr depletion, interfacial oxygen paths, or enhanced inner Al/Ti-rich oxidation.

---

### Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The degradation of additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 high-entropy alloy during elevated-temperature exposure to oxidizing or reducing gases is governed by the formation and growth of oxide scales and internal oxidation, leading to catastrophic failure due to the loss of mechanical integrity.
- **Starting material:** Additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 high-entropy alloy with an fcc matrix
- **What is changed or studied:** Exposure to elevated temperatures (800-1000°C) in oxidizing (air) or reducing (argon-hydrogen) gases
- **Conditions:** Temperature range: 800-1000°C, gas environment: air or argon-hydrogen, exposure time: 1-100 hours
- **Structure through which it acts:** Oxide scales and internal oxidation
- **Mechanism:** Thermally activated diffusion of oxygen and metal ions, leading to the formation and growth of oxide scales and internal oxidation
- **Predicted outcome:** Catastrophic failure due to the loss of mechanical integrity, characterized by a significant decrease in tensile strength and ductility
- **Compared with (baseline):** As-fabricated material with no exposure to elevated temperatures or corrosive gases
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature: 800-1000°C, exposure time: 1-100 hours, gas environment: air or argon-hydrogen
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact mechanism of oxide scale formation and growth, as well as the role of internal oxidation, is not well understood and requires further investigation
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled exposure of additively manufactured Al0.2Co1.5CrFeNi1.5Ti0.3 high-entropy alloy to elevated temperatures (800-1000°C) in oxidizing (air) or reducing (argon-hydrogen) gases, with characterization of microstructure and mechanical properties before and after exposure
  - *Measurements:* Microstructure characterization (SEM, TEM, XRD); Mechanical property testing (tensile testing, hardness testing)
  - *Falsified if:* If no significant degradation or catastrophic failure is observed after exposure to elevated temperatures in oxidizing or reducing gases, the hypothesis is falsified

# Q03

## The research question

> Starting from a eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies, what single intervention could overcome limited room-temperature tensile ductility and brittleness while retaining or increasing strength? The hypothesis should address the tendency for dislocation pile-ups near internal boundaries to produce local stress concentrations, microcracking, and catastrophic damage during straining. Specify the intervention, its mechanism, the predicted strength and ductility relative to the untreated starting material, the applicable testing and operating conditions, supporting prior evidence, and a falsification experiment.

---

### Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Applying a single high-pressure, high-temperature treatment to homogenized Al0.7CoCrFeNi will replace its crack-prone lamellar architecture with a coherent hierarchical pattern, thereby approximately doubling both room-temperature tensile strength and ductility rather than trading one property for the other.
- **Starting material:** The study will start from homogenized eutectic Al0.7CoCrFeNi containing dual-phase FCC/B2 lamellar colonies. The softer FCC and harder B2 phases will initially be separated by largely incoherent internal interfaces, where dislocation pile-ups would promote local stress concentrations, interfacial microcracking, and brittle failure.
- **What is changed or studied:** The alloy will receive one high-pressure and high-temperature treatment in the solid state, followed by cooling and decompression.
- **Conditions:** Treatment will be conducted at approximately 6 GPa and 1470 K. Mechanical performance will be evaluated under room-temperature uniaxial tension, including interrupted testing to strains approaching 35%.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The treatment is expected to transform the dual-phase lamellae into a hierarchically patterned FCC/B2 microstructure with coherent interfaces while preserving the two-phase architecture.
- **Mechanism:** Coherent interfaces should permit preferential transfer of dislocations from the softer FCC phase into the harder B2 phase, reducing pile-ups and associated stress concentrations. The hierarchical pattern should also activate complementary dislocation slip, stacking faults, microbands, and deformation twins. Together, these processes would distribute strain more uniformly between phases, delay plastic instability, and replace catastrophic interfacial crack propagation with spatially distributed microcracking.
- **Predicted outcome:** The treated alloy should reach a fracture strength of about 1.1-1.2 GPa and a uniform tensile elongation of roughly 35-40% at room temperature. Both properties would be approximately twice those of the untreated alloy, while fracture would shift from elongated interfacial cracks toward isolated, distributed microcracks.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The comparator will be the untreated homogenized or conventionally cast eutectic alloy, which would retain strong-yet-brittle FCC/B2 lamellae, with strength and elongation roughly half the predicted treated values.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The synergy is expected only when pressure near 6 GPa at about 1470 K induces the coherent hierarchical pattern. Similar-temperature hot isostatic pressing or thermomechanical treatment at substantially lower pressure should not provide the same combined improvement.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty is whether pressure-temperature processing will reproducibly create sufficient interfacial coherence throughout the bulk alloy to enable phase-to-phase dislocation transfer.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare untreated and high-pressure-treated specimens in room-temperature tensile tests, including interruption near 35% strain and post-fracture microstructural examination.
  - *Measurements:* Fracture strength and uniform tensile elongation; FCC- and B2-phase geometrically necessary dislocation densities by EBSD; Fracture-surface morphology and crack distribution
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if treatment fails to produce approximately 1.1 GPa strength with about 35% elongation, or if phase-specific dislocation densities do not converge near 35% strain and elongated interfacial cracks remain dominant.

---

### Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Introduction of nano-scale precipitates within the lamellar colonies of a eutectic high-entropy alloy can overcome limited room-temperature tensile ductility and brittleness while retaining or increasing strength.
- **Starting material:** Eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies
- **What is changed or studied:** Introduction of nano-scale precipitates
- **Conditions:** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile testing
- **Structure through which it acts:** Nano-scale precipitates
- **Mechanism:** The nano-scale precipitates can act as obstacles to dislocation movement, reducing the tendency for dislocation pile-ups near internal boundaries and thereby decreasing local stress concentrations, microcracking, and catastrophic damage during straining.
- **Predicted outcome:** Retained or increased strength and improved tensile ductility relative to the untreated starting material
- **Compared with (baseline):** Untreated eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical microstructure of dual-phase lamellar colonies
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Room temperature, uniaxial tensile testing
- **Main uncertainty:** The effectiveness of the nano-scale precipitates in improving ductility and strength may depend on factors such as their size, distribution, and composition, which are not specified in this hypothesis.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Uniaxial tensile testing of the untreated and nano-precipitate-introduced alloys at room temperature
  - *Measurements:* Tensile strength; Tensile ductility; Microstructure characterization
  - *Falsified if:* If the introduction of nano-scale precipitates does not result in improved tensile ductility and retained or increased strength relative to the untreated alloy, the hypothesis is falsified.

---

### Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** A targeted post-solidification annealing treatment that selectively disrupts the coherent nature of the lamellar interphase boundaries can reduce dislocation pile-ups, thereby alleviating local stress concentrations and microcracking while retaining (or even modestly increasing) the alloy’s strength.
- **Starting material:** A eutectic high-entropy alloy with a hierarchical dual-phase lamellar colony microstructure in which relatively coherent lamellar interfaces promote dislocation pile-ups that lead to local stress concentrations and brittle failure at room temperature.
- **What is changed or studied:** Apply an optimized thermal annealing treatment (at a temperature and duration calibrated to induce controlled boundary decohesion or increased boundary incoherency without changing the overall phase composition) to modify the structure of the lamellar boundaries.
- **Conditions:** Room-temperature tensile testing under standard strain rates, ensuring that the treatment does not alter the high-temperature operational or processing conditions of the alloy.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The modified (less coherent) interlamellar boundaries that are more effective in absorbing or dispersing dislocations instead of allowing pile-ups.
- **Mechanism:** By inducing a controlled degree of atomic-scale disorder at the lamellar boundaries, the annealing treatment creates interfaces that are better able to absorb dislocations and mitigate pile-up formation. This reduces local stress concentrations that normally trigger microcrack nucleation, thereby improving ductility. Simultaneously, the retained dual-phase lamellar morphology preserves the intrinsic strength contributed by the eutectic structure.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to the untreated condition, the annealed alloy is predicted to display a noticeable increase in room-temperature tensile ductility (due to more diffuse dislocation dispersion) while maintaining, or slightly increasing, tensile strength (as the overall phase morphology is conserved).
- **Compared with (baseline):** The original eutectic high-entropy alloy exhibits limited ductility and brittleness stemming from dislocation accumulation at coherent internal boundaries.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The improvement in ductility and retention of strength is contingent on precisely controlling the annealing parameters; over-annealing might excessively relax the boundaries and decrease strength, while under-annealing would not yield the desired reduction in dislocation pile-ups.
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact annealing temperature and duration required to optimize the boundary incoherency without adversely affecting phase stability and strength enhancement remains to be determined, and there is some uncertainty regarding the sensitivity of the microstructure to slight changes in these parameters.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of alloy samples: one subjected to the proposed optimized annealing treatment and one left untreated. Conduct systematic room-temperature tensile tests while using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and fractography to assess dislocation distribution and crack nucleation sites.
  - *Measurements:* Stress-strain curves to quantify tensile strength and ductility; TEM images to evaluate the degree of boundary incoherency and dislocation distribution; Fractographic analysis to assess initiation and propagation of microcracks
  - *Falsified if:* If the annealed samples do not show a statistically significant reduction in dislocation pile-ups or if there is no measurable improvement in ductility (or a reduction in strength) compared to the untreated samples under identical testing conditions, then the hypothesis that the boundary modification reduces local stress concentrations without sacrificing strength is falsified.

# Q04

## The research question

> For a multi-phase eutectic high-entropy alloy exposed to simultaneous wear and corrosion at elevated temperature, what single mechanistic hypothesis could explain the accelerated coupled degradation and why it occurs? Specify a controlled comparison capable of distinguishing the proposed explanation, predict the changes in total degradation and the individual wear and corrosion responses relative to a matched baseline, define the relevant boundary conditions, identify supporting evidence, and give a clear falsification criterion.

---

### Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Synergistic effects between wear and corrosion at elevated temperatures accelerate degradation in multi-phase eutectic high-entropy alloys due to the formation of micro-galvanic cells and mechanical disruption of the protective oxide layer.
- **Starting material:** Multi-phase eutectic high-entropy alloy with a polished surface
- **What is changed or studied:** Simultaneous wear and corrosion exposure at elevated temperature
- **Conditions:** Elevated temperature (500°C), wear rate of 10 mm/s, and corrosive environment (3.5% NaCl solution)
- **Structure through which it acts:** Formation of micro-galvanic cells and mechanical disruption of the protective oxide layer
- **Mechanism:** Synergistic effects between wear and corrosion
- **Predicted outcome:** Accelerated coupled degradation, with a 30% increase in total degradation, a 20% increase in wear rate, and a 40% increase in corrosion rate relative to the baseline
- **Compared with (baseline):** Single-phase wear or corrosion exposure at room temperature
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range: 400-600°C, wear rate range: 5-15 mm/s, corrosive environment: 1-5% NaCl solution
- **Main uncertainty:** Limited understanding of the specific micro-galvanic cell formation and mechanical disruption mechanisms
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled comparison of simultaneous wear and corrosion exposure at elevated temperature versus single-phase wear or corrosion exposure at room temperature
  - *Measurements:* Total degradation; Wear rate; Corrosion rate
  - *Falsified if:* If the total degradation, wear rate, and corrosion rate do not increase by at least 20% under simultaneous wear and corrosion exposure at elevated temperature compared to the baseline, the hypothesis is falsified

---

### Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Simultaneous wear removes the protective oxide films at the interphase (eutectic) boundaries of the high-entropy alloy, which then become active sites for accelerated galvanic corrosion. This locally enhanced corrosion degrades the interphase cohesion, further increasing the susceptibility to wear. Together, these feedback processes lead to accelerated overall degradation.
- **Starting material:** A multi-phase eutectic high-entropy alloy exhibiting thermally-stabilized, protective oxide films that form on its phase surfaces under ambient or mildly aggressive conditions.
- **What is changed or studied:** Expose identical alloy specimens to combined mechanical wear and a corrosive environment at elevated temperature versus exposure to the corrosive environment alone (with wear minimized or controlled to preserve the oxide film).
- **Conditions:** An elevated temperature environment (e.g., around 600°C) combined with a corrosive medium and controlled mechanical abrasion that is sufficiently aggressive to erode the oxide layers but within service limits.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The interphase boundaries of the eutectic structure, which serve as galvanic couples once their protective films are compromised.
- **Mechanism:** Wear-induced removal of the protective oxide films at interphase boundaries exposes reactive metal surfaces. This exposure creates local galvanic cells due to inherent potential differences between the phases. The resulting accelerated corrosion weakens the microstructural interfaces, increasing susceptibility to further wear, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop of coupled degradation.
- **Predicted outcome:** Relative to a baseline where corrosion proceeds without significant mechanical film removal, the coupled environment is predicted to show an increased overall degradation (i.e., higher weight loss), with more pronounced wear tracks and deeper localized corrosion pits at interphase regions.
- **Compared with (baseline):** An exposure condition where the alloy is subjected solely to the corrosive environment at the same temperature without applied mechanical wear (or with minimal wear that does not disrupt the protective oxide), serving as the reference state for coupled versus independent degradation processes.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The coupling effect is expected only when the applied wear rate exceeds the oxide film reformation rate at elevated temperature. Below this threshold, or if the corrosion environment is not sufficiently aggressive to establish a galvanic couple upon film removal, the accelerated degradation mechanism may not dominate.
- **Main uncertainty:** The precise quantitative contributions of wear versus corrosion and the critical wear rate necessary for disrupting the oxide layer remain uncertain. Additionally, the variation in interphase potentials within the multi-phase eutectic microstructure may yield variable effects across different material compositions.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of identical multi-phase eutectic high-entropy alloy specimens. Subject one set to simultaneous wear and corrosion (using a controlled abrasion system in a corrosive, high-temperature environment) and the other set to corrosion only (ensuring that the oxide film remains largely intact). Periodically characterize surface damage, measure weight loss, and assess corrosion pit depths, with special attention to the interphase regions.
  - *Measurements:* Weight loss over time; Microscopic (SEM) imaging of interphase boundaries; Corrosion pit depth and distribution analysis; Surface profilometry of wear tracks
  - *Falsified if:* If no statistically significant difference in the degree of interphase degradation (or overall weight loss and pit depth) is observed between the specimens subjected to simultaneous wear and corrosion and the corrosion-only specimens (when controlling for oxide film presence), then the hypothesis that wear-induced oxide removal is responsible for enhanced galvanic corrosion and accelerated degradation is falsified.

---

### Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** Accelerated high-temperature tribo-corrosion will arise from repeated formation, fracture, and removal of discontinuous oxide/tribo-layer patches, rather than from corrosion or mechanical wear acting independently. AlCoCrFeNi2.1 should resist this coupling better than duplex stainless steel 2205 because its thermally stable lamellar microstructure should maintain homogeneous Cr availability and rapid passive-film repair.

## The research question

> Single-phase fcc FeCoNi has high saturation magnetization but a low coercive field, and reported single-phase fcc high-entropy alloys derived from it have coercive fields no greater than 100 Oe in the reported magnetic measurements. What single intervention could overcome this coercivity limitation? ### Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Rapid quenching of nominally equiatomic FeCoNiTi will stabilize a ferromagnetic fcc plus hexagonal C14 Laves microstructure whose phase coupling and interfaces will raise the coercive field far beyond the approximately 100 Oe ceiling expected for single-phase fcc FeCoNi-based high-entropy alloys.
- **Starting material:** The target material will be as-cast FeCoNiTi with a nominal Fe:Co:Ni:Ti ratio of 1:1:1:1. A compositionally matched, C14-dominant Fe27Co27Ni17Ti29 alloy will serve as a phase-specific comparator.
- **What is changed or studied:** Rapid solidification on a water-cooled copper hearth after arc melting will be used as the single intervention to retain coexisting fcc and C14 Laves phases rather than a single equilibrium phase.
- **Conditions:** Magnetic behavior will be evaluated through 400 K, with higher-temperature magnetic ordering assessed for the proposed fcc transition near 1100 K. Spin-polarized density-of-states calculations will evaluate the electronic and magnetic states of both phases.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The mediator will be coexistence and interfacial contact between an fcc region of approximately Fe21Co25Ni32Ti22 and a C14 region of approximately Fe27Co27Ni17Ti29, including spin-canted components at or near the dual-phase structure.
- **Mechanism:** Antiferromagnetic coupling between the Fe/Co/Ni and Ti sublattices will reduce saturation magnetization but permit high-temperature ferromagnetic ordering in the fcc phase. The fcc orbital moment, although small in an isolated fcc phase, will contribute to coercivity when coupled to the C14 phase and spin-canted components, enabling domain-wall pinning. Transitions near 1100 K and 170 K will originate from fcc, whereas a transition near 210 K will arise from C14.
- **Predicted outcome:** The dual-phase alloy will reach a coercive field of roughly 650-700 Oe at 400 K, versus no more than about 100 Oe for single-phase fcc FeCoNi or a matched fcc derivative. It will also show three ferromagnetic transitions near 1100, 210, and 170 K.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The baseline will be single-phase fcc Fe-Co-Ni-based alloys, which should have high saturation magnetization, coercivity at or below approximately 100 Oe, and limited orbital-moment-assisted pinning.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The enhancement will require the specified fcc-C14 coexistence, segregation, and antiferromagnetic sublattice coupling. Neither an isolated fcc phase nor a C14-dominant or pure C14 variant should reproduce the combined high coercivity and three-transition response.
- **Main uncertainty:** Rapid solidification may change phase fractions or interfacial connectivity enough to weaken pinning even if both phases remain present.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare rapidly quenched dual-phase FeCoNiTi with C14-dominant Fe27Co27Ni17Ti29 and a single-phase fcc FeCoNi-based control under matched magnetic testing.

### Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** By subjecting single-phase fcc FeCoNi to a controlled annealing process that induces nanoscale precipitation of an ordered, high-anisotropy phase (e.g., an L1₀-like phase), the magnetic coercivity can be significantly increased while largely preserving the high saturation magnetization.
- **Starting material:** Single-phase fcc FeCoNi (or a closely related high-entropy alloy derivative) with a coercivity below 100 Oe.
- **What is changed or studied:** Thermal treatment (annealing) designed to induce controlled nanoscale precipitation of an ordered phase within the fcc matrix.
- **Conditions:** The annealing process must be performed under conditions (specific temperature and time windows) that promote the formation of nanoscale, ordered precipitates without full decomposition of the fcc phase. The composition remains essentially that of FeCoNi, and the process should be conducted in an inert or reducing atmosphere to prevent oxidation.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Nanoscale precipitates (e.g., L1₀-like ordered regions) embedded within the fcc matrix that create local lattice distortions and act as pinning centers for domain walls.
- **Mechanism:** The formation of ordered precipitates introduces additional magnetocrystalline anisotropy due to the tetragonal distortion inherent to the ordered phase. This enhanced anisotropy increases the energy barrier for domain wall motion, thereby raising the coercive field.
- **Predicted outcome:** An increase in coercivity beyond the &lt;100 Oe level observed in the single-phase fcc alloy, with minimal compromise to the high saturation magnetization characteristic of the base material.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Coercivity and saturation magnetization measurements of the initial single-phase fcc FeCoNi alloy prior to annealing.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis applies as long as the alloy composition is maintained and the annealing treatment is optimized such that only a minority fraction (nanoscale precipitates) forms without triggering a full phase transformation or significantly reducing the ferromagnetic volume fraction.
- **Main uncertainty:** Precise control over precipitate size, distribution, volume fraction, and the corresponding quantitative improvement in coercivity are uncertain and may vary with subtle changes in annealing parameters. Additionally, the exact nature of the ordered phase (and its compatibility with the base fcc matrix) requires further experimental calibration.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare identical samples of single-phase fcc FeCoNi. Subject half of the samples to the designed annealing treatment (followed by rapid cooling or controlled cooling to retain the nanoscale precipitates) while keeping the other half as controls. Use high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) to confirm the presence of nanoscale ordered precipitates in the annealed samples. Measure the magnetic hysteresis loops using vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) or SQUID magnetometry to determine coercivity and saturation magnetization.
  - *Measurements:* Presence, size, and distribution of precipitates (via HRTEM and XRD); Magnetic hysteresis loops (to extract coercivity and saturation magnetization)
  - *Falsified if:* If the annealed samples do not show a statistically significant increase in coercivity over 100 Oe compared to the control samples, or if the formation of nanoscale ordered precipitates is not observed, then the hypothesis will be falsified.

### Hypothesis C
