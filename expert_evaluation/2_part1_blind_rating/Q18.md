## The research question

> For single-phase ZrB2 ceramic, which requires preparation temperatures above 2300 °C and may lack the strength and structural integrity needed for service, what single intervention could lower the required preparation temperature and reduce the holes and eventual component failure caused by prolonged exposure to a liquid plating bath? State one hypothesis identifying the intervention and its mechanism, predict its performance compared with conventionally prepared single-phase ZrB2, define the relevant preparation and service boundaries, identify supporting prior evidence, and describe a controlled preparation, integrity, and long-duration exposure experiment that could falsify the hypothesis.

The three hypotheses below were written for this question. Read all three, then rate each one on its own merits.

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Employing spark plasma sintering (SPS) to consolidate single‐phase ZrB2 at reduced temperatures will lower the preparation temperature while simultaneously producing a denser ceramic with fewer pores, thereby improving its strength and resistance against degradation from prolonged exposure to a liquid plating bath.
- **Starting material:** Conventional single‐phase ZrB2 prepared via pressureless sintering at temperatures above 2300 °C, characterized by significant porosity and suboptimal mechanical integrity.
- **What is changed or studied:** Replace conventional high‐temperature pressureless sintering with spark plasma sintering (SPS) conducted at a reduced temperature range (approximately 1800–2000 °C) under applied pressure and an inert atmosphere.
- **Conditions:** Sintering under controlled inert atmosphere with SPS parameters that include rapid heating rates and applied pressure (e.g., around 50 MPa), targeting a densification threshold at a temperature lower than the conventional &gt;2300 °C.
- **Structure through which it acts:** A refined microstructure with higher density and reduced residual porosity.
- **Mechanism:** The rapid heating and applied pressure in SPS enhance atomic diffusion and densification at lower temperatures, suppressing excessive grain growth and pore formation. This leads to a microstructure that is more uniform and mechanically robust, which in turn minimizes the formation of holes when exposed to a corrosive liquid plating bath.
- **Predicted outcome:** SPS-processed ZrB2 will exhibit higher density and fewer porosity defects compared to conventionally sintered material; as a result, these samples are predicted to have improved mechanical strength and better resistance to the corrosive effects of a liquid plating bath during long-duration exposure.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Conventionally prepared ZrB2 using pressureless sintering above 2300 °C, which typically results in a porous microstructure with lower mechanical strength and increased vulnerability to liquid-induced degradation.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Preparation: sintering temperatures between approximately 1800–2300 °C under inert conditions using SPS versus &gt;2300 °C with conventional methods. Service: exposure to liquid plating baths under typical operational conditions where existing porosity leads to component failure.
- **Main uncertainty:** While the rapid densification of SPS suggests improved outcomes, the exact degree of reduction in preparation temperature, extent of porosity reduction, and quantitative improvement in mechanical properties remain uncertain without experimental validation. Additionally, process optimization parameters (e.g., pressure levels, heating rates) may influence the final performance.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two series of single-phase ZrB2 samples: one series using conventional pressureless sintering at &gt;2300 °C and a second series using SPS at 1800–2000 °C under controlled conditions (with identical starting powders and compositions). Characterize the microstructure and mechanical properties of both sets to assess density, grain size, and residual porosity. Subsequently, subject the samples to a standardized long-duration liquid plating bath exposure protocol while monitoring for the formation of holes, surface degradation, and mechanical failure.
  - *Measurements:* Density assessment (e.g., Archimedes method); Microstructural analysis via SEM and/or TEM to quantify porosity and grain structure; Mechanical strength testing (e.g., three-point bending or flexural strength); Surface integrity evaluation and defect quantification pre- and post-plating bath exposure
  - *Falsified if:* If the SPS-processed samples do not exhibit a statistically significant improvement in density and mechanical strength, or if they do not show a measurable reduction in pore formation and improved resistance to degradation after liquid plating bath exposure compared to the conventionally sintered samples, then the hypothesis is falsified.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The addition of a secondary phase, such as SiC nanoparticles, to the ZrB2 ceramic can lower the required preparation temperature and improve its structural integrity by enhancing densification and reducing porosity.
- **Starting material:** Single-phase ZrB2 ceramic powder
- **What is changed or studied:** Addition of SiC nanoparticles
- **Conditions:** Preparation temperature above 2000 °C, exposure to liquid plating bath
- **Structure through which it acts:** SiC nanoparticles
- **Mechanism:** Enhanced densification and reduced porosity due to the presence of SiC nanoparticles
- **Predicted outcome:** Improved structural integrity and reduced component failure
- **Compared with (baseline):** Conventional single-phase ZrB2 ceramic prepared at temperatures above 2300 °C
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Preparation temperature range: 2000-2300 °C, exposure duration: up to 100 hours
- **Main uncertainty:** Effect of SiC nanoparticle size and distribution on the densification and porosity of ZrB2 ceramic
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled preparation and exposure experiment with varying SiC nanoparticle concentrations and preparation temperatures
  - *Measurements:* Density; Porosity; Structural integrity; Exposure duration
  - *Falsified if:* If the addition of SiC nanoparticles does not result in a significant reduction in porosity and improvement in structural integrity compared to conventional single-phase ZrB2 ceramic, the hypothesis is falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** Adding 30% equimolar AlFeNiCoCr high-entropy-alloy binder to ZrB2 and spark-plasma-sintering near 1500 °C would lower the required consolidation temperature from above 2300 °C while improving structural integrity, wear resistance, and resistance to prolonged molten-aluminum attack.
- **Starting material:** The starting material would be a milled and dried mixture of ZrB2 ceramic powder and 30% AlFeNiCoCr binder formed from equimolar Al, Fe, Ni, Co, and Cr.
- **What is changed or studied:** The mixture would be consolidated by spark plasma sintering, with 1500 °C proposed as the optimum and 1300, 1400, and 1600 °C used as processing comparisons.
- **Conditions:** Sintering would occur under vacuum over 1300–1600 °C. Service testing would include immersion in molten Al at 700 °C for up to 12 days and room-temperature sliding against Si3N4 under a 10 N load.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Near 1500 °C, the liquid alloy binder would uniformly fill pores and strongly connect ZrB2 grains, producing a compact microstructure with approximately 98% relative density. At 1600 °C, binder overflow or loss would instead increase porosity and reduce density to about 95%.
- **Mechanism:** Rising temperature through 1500 °C would improve binder fluidity, pore filling, intergranular bonding, and densification. The resulting continuous interfaces would limit molten-Al penetration, ZrB2-grain detachment, crack propagation, and abrasive wear. Excess temperature would remove binder from the compact, weaken grain bonding, and create pathways through which Al could penetrate and convert the metallic binder into Al-rich reaction products.
- **Predicted outcome:** The 1500 °C cermet would reach approximately 1900 HV hardness and 6.5–7 MPa·m^1/2 fracture toughness, about 1.4 times the roughly 4.5 MPa·m^1/2 value of pure ZrB2. It would have the lowest 12-day molten-Al corrosion rate, on the order of 10^-3 mm/h, versus roughly 3–5 × 10^-3 mm/h for the other temperatures, and the lowest wear volume at about 0.10 mm^3 with a stable friction coefficient near 0.46.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The principal baselines would be the same 30%-binder cermet sintered at 1300, 1400, or 1600 °C, with conventionally prepared single-phase ZrB2 serving as the preparation-temperature and fracture-toughness reference.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis would apply to the specified 30% binder composition, vacuum spark plasma sintering near 1500 °C, molten Al at 700 °C for no more than 12 days, and the stated room-temperature wear condition. It would not predict continued improvement at 1600 °C because binder loss should reverse densification.
- **Main uncertainty:** Thickness-loss sensitivity and nonuniform penetration could place the true corrosion rate anywhere within the 10^-4–10^-3 mm/h order-of-magnitude range.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare compositionally identical cermets at 1300, 1400, 1500, and 1600 °C, alongside pure ZrB2, then compare initial integrity, room-temperature wear, and molten-Al exposure at 700 °C for 4, 8, and 12 days.
  - *Measurements:* Relative density, hardness, and indentation fracture toughness; SEM/EDS and XRD assessment of pores, interfaces, phases, and corrosion layers; Thickness-loss corrosion rate, wear volume, and friction coefficient
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if the 1500 °C cermet did not combine the highest density and toughness with significantly lower 12-day corrosion and wear than the 1300, 1400, and 1600 °C cermets, or if microscopy did not link that performance to uniform pore filling and strong ZrB2–binder interfaces.
