## The research question (same as in Part 1)

> For initially solid FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles exposed to the premelting and melting regime, the limited understanding of their thermal response prevents clear definition of their usable temperature range and behavior during high-temperature synthesis. What single testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain what governs the onset and progression of their phase transition and why? Specify a controlled comparison, predicted observations relative to the starting baseline, relevant boundary conditions, supporting evidence from prior nanoparticle studies, and a result that would falsify the hypothesis.

Enhanced diffusion does not produce persistent elemental clusters or precipitates during the heating ramp. |
| **Outcome** | Relative to smaller nanoparticles, increasing particle radius raises the melting temperature toward the bulk limit. Relative to a liquid-shell/surface-premelting explanation, the nanoparticles are predicted to follow an approximately inverse-radius two-phase melting relation with no appreciable liquid-shell contribution; Fe and Cu should become mobile first, but the initially random elemental distribution should remain largely unclustered. |
| **Baseline** | The principal baselines were the fitted bulk melting limit of 2184 K, the initially random equiatomic FCC distribution, and conventional surface-melting/liquid-shell models in which unbound surface atoms dominate nanoparticle melting. |
| **Boundary** | The reported behavior holds across the simulated ultrasmall FeNiCrCoCu nanoparticles, nominally about 5–40 Å in radius, under the specified gradual thermal ramp. Preferential Fe/Cu mobility was nearly negligible at R = 5 Å and became clearer with increasing size. Slight short-range CuNi/CuCo clustering and CuCr repulsion appeared at R = 7.5–10 Å, but diminished for larger particles; the conclusions are limited to the employed interatomic potential, composition, sizes, and heating protocol. |
| **Key measurement** | Melting was identified from potential-energy-versus-temperature curves and fitted against radius and reciprocal radius. Element-resolved mean-square displacement tested preferential mobility; pair-correlation functions, Warren–Cowley parameters, atomic visualizations, common-neighbor analysis, and polyhedral-template matching tested clustering or crystalline subdomains; element-resolved potential energy and local von Mises shear strain tested the proposed energetic and spatial origin of premelting. |
| **Actual result** | Melting temperature increased strongly with particle size and approached saturation near R = 40 Å. The size fit gave an exponent c = -0.978, close to the classical -1 inverse-radius dependence, with a fitted bulk melting point T0 = 2184 K; the reciprocal-radius fit was consistent with C = 1. Fe and Cu exhibited enhanced low-temperature mobility, with Fe showing an abrupt potential-energy/MSD increase around 1000 K. Only a few high-strain surface layers were present near 900 K, providing no evidence for a substantial liquid shell. Pair correlations showed no new peaks during heating, Warren–Cowley values were generally near zero, and structural analyses found no stable elemental clusters, precipitation, or mono-element crystalline domains, essentially independent of nanoparticle size. |

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** The onset and progression of phase transition in FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles is governed by the thermally induced surface segregation of the low–melting-point component (Cu). This segregation reduces the local melting temperature at the nanoparticle surface, initiating premelting that propagates inwards as the temperature rises.
- **Starting material:** Solid, as‐synthesized FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles with an initially homogeneous elemental distribution.
- **What is changed or studied:** Controlled heating from sub-premelting temperatures through the premelting and into the melting regime.
- **Conditions:** Experiments conducted under an inert atmosphere with a constant heating rate and controlled pressure to prevent oxidation and ensure reproducibility.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Localized surface enrichment of Cu that creates regions with lower melting points.
- **Mechanism:** As temperature increases, thermal energy drives Cu atoms to segregate to the nanoparticle surface due to its lower melting point relative to the other elements. This leads to the formation of Cu-enriched sites that melt at temperatures below the bulk melting point. Once these sites are established, premelting ensues at the surface and progresses inward, eventually leading to complete melting of the nanoparticle.
- **Predicted outcome:** In situ observations (e.g., via TEM) will reveal that melting begins at the nanoparticle surfaces, particularly in regions where EDS mapping shows Cu enrichment. Nanoparticles with a pre-annealed (and thus more homogenized) surface are expected to show a delayed or higher onset temperature for premelting/melting compared to untreated particles.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Nanoparticles maintained at temperatures far below the onset of any premelting, exhibiting uniform elemental distribution and solid crystalline structure.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** This hypothesis is expected to hold for nanoparticles with sizes that permit sufficient atomic mobility for segregation; extremely small particles or those subjected to extremely rapid heating may not follow the same behavior.
- **Main uncertainty:** The quantitative extent of Cu segregation necessary to trigger surface premelting and the precise temperature shift induced by this segregation remain to be determined.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of nanoparticles: one left as-synthesized and one pre-annealed to minimize surface segregation. Heat both sets in an inert atmosphere using identical heating rates while monitoring structural changes using in situ TEM and EDS mapping. Additionally, perform differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) to record the onset temperature of melting.
  - *Measurements:* TEM imaging to observe the initiation of surface premelting; EDS mapping to assess the extent of Cu segregation at the surface; DSC to determine the temperature at which endothermic events indicative of melting occur
  - *Falsified if:* If the pre-annealed (homogenized) nanoparticles do not show a statistically significant delay or a higher onset temperature for melting relative to as-synthesized nanoparticles, or if surface-localized premelting is observed without corresponding Cu enrichment, the hypothesis is falsified.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The onset and progression of phase transition in FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles are governed by the competition between the reduction of surface energy and the increase in internal energy due to thermal expansion.
- **Starting material:** Solid FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles
- **What is changed or studied:** Exposure to premelting and melting regime temperatures
- **Conditions:** High-temperature synthesis conditions
- **Structure through which it acts:** Surface energy and internal energy balance
- **Mechanism:** Thermal expansion and surface energy reduction compete to drive phase transition
- **Predicted outcome:** A gradual increase in particle size and a decrease in surface area as the particles undergo phase transition, with a possible emergence of a liquid phase at higher temperatures
- **Compared with (baseline):** Initial solid nanoparticle state with defined surface area and size distribution
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range of premelting and melting regime, specific to FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles
- **Main uncertainty:** Lack of specific data on the thermal properties of FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles, particularly in the premelting and melting regime
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* In situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or X-ray diffraction (XRD) to monitor the phase transition of FeNiCrCoCu high-entropy-alloy nanoparticles during heating
  - *Measurements:* Particle size distribution; Surface area; Phase composition
  - *Falsified if:* If the particles undergo a sudden, discontinuous phase transition without significant changes in surface area or particle size, the hypothesis would be falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** Melting of equiatomic FeNiCrCoCu nanoparticles will be governed primarily by radius-dependent competition between solid and liquid phases, coupled to element-specific diffusion from both the surface and core, rather than by formation of a stable liquid surface shell. Fe and Cu will initiate premelting mobility because chemically disordered FCC environments will place Fe at relatively high local energy and pressure, while Cu will have intrinsically favorable mobility associated with its lower elemental melting point.
- **Starting material:** Spherical, initially solid nanoparticles will be cut from a random equiatomic Fe–Ni–Cr–Co–Cu FCC lattice with an approximately 3.5 Å lattice parameter, energy-minimized, and relaxed to about 300 K.
- **What is changed or studied:** Classical molecular-dynamics simulations using an embedded-atom-method potential will compare nanoparticles of increasing radius while heating them through premelting and complete melting.
- **Conditions:** Particles with radii of approximately 5–40 Å will be heated gradually from 300 to 2500 K in roughly 20 K increments, with linear and angular momentum removed.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The proposed mediator will be preferential early Fe and Cu mobility within the chemically disordered FCC structure. Fe local potential energy should exceed that of the other elements by about 0.5 eV. Near 900 K, high-displacement and high-strain atoms should remain confined to only two or three surface layers, while mobility subsequently propagates into the core.
- **Mechanism:** Elevated Fe local energy or pressure and the relatively low elemental melting point of Cu will enhance their diffusion. This diffusion will promote solid–liquid competition throughout the nanoparticle without producing a persistent liquid shell, elemental precipitates, or stable mono-element crystalline domains.
- **Predicted outcome:** Melting temperature will rise strongly with radius and approach a bulk limit near 2200 K. Its size dependence will be approximately inverse-radius, with an exponent near -1. Fe and Cu will become mobile first, including a marked Fe mobility and energy change near 1000 K, but the initially random elemental distribution will remain largely unclustered.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Comparisons will use the random equiatomic FCC starting distribution, a fitted bulk melting limit near 2200 K, and conventional surface-premelting models in which unbound surface atoms form a substantial liquid shell.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis will apply to equiatomic particles of roughly 5–40 Å under the specified gradual heating protocol and chosen potential. Fe/Cu selectivity may be weak at 5 Å. Minor short-range CuNi or CuCo association and CuCr avoidance may occur around 8–10 Å but should diminish at larger radii.
- **Main uncertainty:** The inferred mechanism and bulk-limit extrapolation may depend on the accuracy of the embedded-atom potential for chemically disordered local environments.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Simulate the radius series under an identical heating ramp and compare inverse-radius melting behavior with the liquid-shell alternative.
  - *Measurements:* Potential energy versus temperature and melting temperature versus reciprocal radius; Element-resolved mean-square displacement and potential energy; Local shear strain and spatial distribution of mobile atoms; Pair correlations, Warren–Cowley parameters, and local structural classifications
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if melting does not scale approximately as inverse radius, or if premelting instead produces a substantial persistent liquid surface shell, stable elemental clustering, or no preferential Fe/Cu mobility.
