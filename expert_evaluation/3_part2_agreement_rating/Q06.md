## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Creep resistance in FCC/BCC Al-Cr-Fe-Ni multilayer coatings would be governed primarily by interface coherency rather than modulation period alone. A semicoherent interface population at an intermediate modulation period would maximize resistance to progressive plastic deformation by blocking dislocations and shifting the rate-controlling process from dislocation slip or climb toward atomic diffusion or interface slip.
- **Starting material:** The starting material would be an as-deposited, approximately 1 μm-thick FCC/BCC Al-Cr-Fe-Ni multilayer coating produced by alternating Al0.2CrFeNi and Al deposition.
- **What is changed or studied:** The modulation period, λ, would be varied among approximately 8, 16, 32, and 80 nm by sputter deposition. This controlled comparison would generate coatings dominated by coherent heterointerfaces at 8 nm, semicoherent heterointerfaces at 16 nm, and grain boundaries at 32–80 nm, where λ exceeds the approximately 20 nm grain diameter.
- **Conditions:** The coatings would undergo room-temperature nanoindentation creep under a maximum load of approximately 5 mN, loading rates spanning 0.01–1 mN/s, and a hold of approximately 100 s. Indentation depth would remain below roughly one-seventh of coating thickness.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The proposed mediator would be FCC/BCC interface coherency. The 16 nm multilayer would contain semicoherent interfaces with misfit dislocations, whereas the 8 nm multilayer would retain continuous atomic planes and the coarser multilayers would become grain-boundary dominated.
- **Mechanism:** Semicoherent interfaces would obstruct glide, promote dislocation pileups, and generate localized stress. Consistent with prior molecular-dynamics and high-resolution microscopy evidence, this stress could induce interfacial amorphization. Free volume, structural disorder, and high-energy crystal–amorphous boundaries would lower atomic-migration barriers, permit rate-limited diffusion or interface slip, and redistribute stress. Coherent interfaces would transmit dislocations more readily, while grain-boundary-dominated coatings would deform mainly through dislocation slip or climb.
- **Predicted outcome:** The 16 nm coating would show the strongest creep resistance and the lowest stress exponent, approximately 1.5–2.5 across loading rates. At 1 mN/s, its creep displacement and steady-state rate would be about 10 nm and 3 × 10^-4 s^-1, compared with roughly 20–25 nm and 4–6 × 10^-4 s^-1 for the principal baselines. The other coatings would generally have stress exponents above 3.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Baselines would be the coherent 8 nm coating and grain-boundary-dominated 32 and 80 nm coatings, for which dislocation-mediated creep would be expected.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis would apply to approximately 1 μm sputtered coatings under room-temperature, 5 mN indentation creep. It would not establish behavior under elevated-temperature or macroscopic creep.
- **Main uncertainty:** Stress-induced amorphization might not occur; semicoherent interfaces alone could instead account for dislocation blocking.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare all four modulation periods under identical indentation creep conditions while independently classifying interface coherency by diffraction and lattice-resolved imaging.
  - *Measurements:* Creep displacement and steady-state strain rate; Stress exponent from ln(strain rate) versus ln(flow stress); Coherent, semicoherent, and grain-boundary interface character
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis would be falsified if the semicoherent 16 nm coating did not produce the minimum stress exponent and improved creep response, or if its exponent remained above 3 and indistinguishable from the coherent and grain-boundary-dominated baselines.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** The dual-phase FCC/BCC high-entropy-alloy coating exhibits enhanced creep resistance because heterophase interfaces between the FCC and BCC regions act as effective barriers to dislocation climb and glide, thereby impeding plastic deformation under continuous loading.
- **Starting material:** A high-entropy alloy protective coating featuring a dual-phase microstructure with distinct FCC and BCC regions that form a network of heterophase boundaries as produced by standard processing.
- **What is changed or studied:** Alter the thermal processing (e.g., annealing or quenching parameters) to refine (or, in a comparison set, coarsen) the distribution and spacing of the FCC/BCC heterophase interfaces.
- **Conditions:** Sustained mechanical loading at temperatures and stress levels where dislocation-mediated creep is the dominant deformation process.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The heterophase interface boundaries between the FCC and BCC grains.
- **Mechanism:** The heterophase boundaries serve as physical obstacles that pin dislocations, increasing the energy barrier for both dislocation climb and glide. This barrier effect delays the progression of plastic deformation (creep) during continuous loading.
- **Predicted outcome:** Under sustained stress, coatings with a refined, uniformly distributed network of heterophase interfaces will display a lower rate of creep strain (i.e., slower accumulation of plastic deformation) compared to coatings with a coarser or less uniform interface structure.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The as-processed dual-phase coating with typical heterophase boundary spacing resulting from standard thermal treatment.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** This mechanism is expected to operate effectively within the stress and temperature regime where dislocation creep is dominant; however, at very high temperatures or extreme stresses (where mechanisms such as grain boundary sliding become prevalent), the interface pinning effect may diminish.
- **Main uncertainty:** The relative magnitude of the interface pinning contribution compared to other possible resistance mechanisms (e.g., grain boundary sliding resistance) is uncertain without further microstructural and mechanical analysis, and the precise quantitative impact remains to be determined.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of coatings: one set processed to produce a refined, uniform network of FCC/BCC interfaces and a second set intentionally processed to generate coarser, less uniform interfaces. Subject both sets to identical creep testing conditions (sustained load at the relevant temperature and stress levels). Use high-resolution microscopy (e.g., TEM) to verify interface characteristics.
  - *Measurements:* Creep strain versus time profiles for both sets; Dislocation distribution and density near heterophase boundaries (via TEM); Interface spacing and morphology characterization
  - *Falsified if:* If the coatings with refined heterophase interfaces do not show a reproducibly lower creep strain rate (i.e., significantly slower plastic deformation) relative to the coarser-interface coatings under the same conditions, then the hypothesis that heterophase interface pinning is the primary mechanism for enhanced creep resistance should be rejected.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The creep resistance of an FCC/BCC high-entropy-alloy protective coating is governed by the formation of a stable oxide layer on its surface, which acts as a barrier to dislocation movement and grain boundary sliding.
- **Starting material:** Polished and cleaned FCC/BCC high-entropy-alloy coating
- **What is changed or studied:** Continuous loading at elevated temperatures
- **Conditions:** Sustained stress in the range of 100-500 MPa and temperatures between 500-800°C
- **Structure through which it acts:** Stable oxide layer formation
- **Mechanism:** Reduced dislocation movement and grain boundary sliding due to oxide layer formation
- **Predicted outcome:** Improved creep resistance compared to the baseline, characterized by reduced steady-state creep rates and increased time to failure
- **Compared with (baseline):** Uncoated or bare FCC/BCC high-entropy-alloy
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature and stress ranges beyond which the oxide layer becomes unstable or the alloy undergoes significant microstructural changes
- **Main uncertainty:** The exact composition and microstructure of the oxide layer, as well as the underlying deformation mechanisms, are not well understood and require further investigation
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare the creep behavior of coated and uncoated FCC/BCC high-entropy-alloy samples under identical loading conditions
  - *Measurements:* Creep strain vs. time; Steady-state creep rate; Time to failure
  - *Falsified if:* If the coated sample exhibits no significant improvement in creep resistance compared to the uncoated sample, the hypothesis is falsified
