## The research question (same as in Part 1)

> For a Mg-containing lightweight high-entropy alloy initially in a non-equilibrium, metastable solid-solution-containing state, what single testable mechanistic hypothesis could explain its subsequent microstructural evolution and the associated retention or loss of corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance? The hypothesis should address the unresolved transformation pathway and kinetics while accounting for the tendency of these alloys to develop intermetallic compounds. Specify an unnamed controlled comparison, predicted observations relative to the initial state, the relevant service-regime boundaries, supporting evidence from reported Mg-containing alloys, and a clear falsification result.

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** Under high-temperature service conditions, the metastable, Mg-containing high-entropy alloy undergoes a diffusion‐driven transformation in which Mg-rich nanoclusters rapidly nucleate and coalesce into intermetallic precipitates. This two‐stage process, initiated by slight short-range ordering and progressing through nucleation and growth, simultaneously alters the alloy’s local chemistry and disrupts the formation/maintenance of a protective passive film, thereby diminishing its corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance.
- **Starting material:** Metastable, non-equilibrium solid-solution state in a Mg-containing lightweight high-entropy alloy, with a homogeneously distributed solid-solution prior to any significant intermetallic precipitation.
- **What is changed or studied:** Thermal exposure (aging) under controlled high-temperature service-regime conditions.
- **Conditions:** Aging at service-relevant temperatures near the upper bound of the expected operating regime (for example, within a temperature window where oxidation resistance is critical, yet diffusional kinetics are accelerated), with environmental exposure to oxidizing and corrosive atmospheres.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Nanoscale Mg-rich clusters that serve as precursors to intermetallic compound nucleation and subsequent growth.
- **Mechanism:** In the elevated temperature service regime, increased diffusion rates promote the segregation of Mg (and accompanying elements) from the metastable solid solution to form short-range ordered clusters. With continued exposure, these clusters act as nucleation sites for the development of intermetallic compounds. The resultant phase separation leads to local chemical depletion of Mg in the matrix and formation of heterogeneous microstructures, which compromise the integrity of the passive film and thereby reduce corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance.
- **Predicted outcome:** Compared to the initial state, aged samples will show the emergence of discrete, Mg-rich intermetallic precipitates by means of microstructural characterization (e.g., via TEM or XRD). This phase transformation will correlate with measurable declines in corrosion resistance (via electrochemical tests) and oxidation performance (via high-temperature cyclic oxidation tests).
- **Compared with (baseline):** The initial, homogeneous metastable solid solution with uniformly distributed alloying elements and robust passive film formation.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Service conditions defined by high-temperature exposure and oxidizing/corrosive atmospheres where diffusion kinetics are accelerated but below the melting point of the alloy, representing the operational limits for corrosion/oxidation resistance.
- **Main uncertainty:** The precise kinetics of Mg segregation and the threshold temperature/time combination required to trigger the intermetallic transformation remain uncertain, as do any potential local compositional variations that might stabilize the passive film despite microstructural changes.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare a batch of Mg-containing high-entropy alloy samples in the as-cast/metastable state. Expose a subset to a controlled thermal aging treatment at service-relevant temperatures in an oxidizing and corrosive atmosphere while maintaining an equivalent control group at ambient temperature. Characterize the microstructure using TEM/SEM and XRD before and after aging, and evaluate corrosion performance using electrochemical tests in a standardized electrolyte, along with high-temperature cyclic oxidation tests.
  - *Measurements:* Microstructural analysis for detection of nanoscale clustering and intermetallic precipitate formation (TEM, XRD); Quantitative measurement of change in corrosion rate (potentiodynamic polarization, EIS); High-temperature oxidation weight gain/loss measurements and surface analysis
  - *Falsified if:* If aging under the prescribed service conditions does not result in the formation of detectable Mg-rich intermetallic precipitates and there is no statistically significant degradation in corrosion and oxidation resistance compared to the baseline, then the hypothesized mechanism is falsified.

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** A metastable BCC MgAlTiVFeCo powder compact will undergo diffusion-enabled precipitation during spark plasma sintering, with a transformation optimum near 1000 °C. At this optimum, densification and uniformly distributed oxide barrier phases will outweigh the alloy’s competing tendency to form intermetallic compounds, thereby preserving corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance.
- **Starting material:** The starting material will be ball-milled MgAlTiVFeCo powder containing a simple, non-equilibrium BCC solid solution.
- **What is changed or studied:** The powder will be consolidated by spark plasma sintering, with an unnamed controlled comparison between material sintered near 1000 °C and otherwise comparable material sintered at lower temperatures.
- **Conditions:** Sintering will span approximately 800–1000 °C. Oxidation behavior will be evaluated in air at 900 °C, and corrosion behavior will be evaluated in 3.5 wt% NaCl.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Near 1000 °C, the microstructure should approach 97% relative density and develop uniformly distributed barrier phases, including AlV2O4, FeAl2O4, and FeTiO3. These phases should form a more continuous protective structure than at lower sintering temperatures.
- **Mechanism:** The combined temperature and pressure should provide sufficient diffusion activity for densification, precipitation, and growth of a continuous passive film. The film should raise polarization resistance and impede chloride penetration, shifting corrosion toward localized penetration-defense behavior. Oxidation should remain diffusion-controlled, follow a parabolic law, and exhibit two growth regimes.
- **Predicted outcome:** Material sintered near 1000 °C should reach about 97% density and roughly 1240 HV, while outperforming lower-temperature material in oxidation and corrosion resistance. Expected biphasic parabolic oxidation constants will be approximately 0.04 and 0.1 mg^2·cm^-4·s^-1. The corrosion potential should shift positively to about -0.44 V, with current density near 1×10^-6 A·cm^-2.
- **Compared with (baseline):** Compacts sintered at approximately 850–950 °C should remain less dense than 97%, softer, and less corrosion- and oxidation-resistant because their barrier phases and passive films will be less effective.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Below about 800 °C, insufficient sintering driving force should limit density, potentially to near or below 80%. Above 1000 °C, magnesium volatilization should undermine composition and performance; thus the hypothesis will apply only within this processing window and to oxidation near 900 °C and chloride exposure near 3.5 wt% NaCl.
- **Main uncertainty:** Competing intermetallic-compound formation could consume constituents needed for protective oxide precipitation and alter the proposed transformation pathway or its kinetics.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare otherwise equivalent MgAlTiVFeCo compacts sintered near 1000 °C and at lower temperatures, then expose them to 900 °C air and 3.5 wt% NaCl.
  - *Measurements:* Two-regime parabolic oxidation rate constants; Corrosion potential and corrosion current density; Relative density, hardness, and barrier-phase distribution
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if the 1000 °C condition does not produce a denser barrier-phase microstructure, parabolic biphasic oxidation, and a more positive corrosion potential with lower current density than the lower-temperature comparison.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** The microstructural evolution of a Mg-containing lightweight high-entropy alloy is driven by the precipitation of intermetallic compounds, which alters its corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance.
- **Starting material:** Non-equilibrium, metastable solid-solution-containing state
- **What is changed or studied:** Aging treatment at elevated temperatures
- **Conditions:** Controlled atmosphere, varying temperature and time
- **Structure through which it acts:** Precipitation of intermetallic compounds
- **Mechanism:** Nucleation and growth of intermetallic phases, leading to changes in the alloy's microstructure and composition
- **Predicted outcome:** Retention or loss of corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance, depending on the nature and distribution of the precipitated intermetallic compounds
- **Compared with (baseline):** Initial non-equilibrium state
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature and time limits for precipitation of intermetallic compounds, beyond which the alloy's properties degrade
- **Main uncertainty:** Lack of detailed understanding of the transformation pathway and kinetics, as well as the specific role of Mg in the alloy's microstructural evolution
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Controlled comparison of aged and unaged samples, with characterization of microstructure, composition, and properties
  - *Measurements:* Microstructural analysis (e.g., TEM, SEM); Compositional analysis (e.g., EDS, XRD); Corrosion and high-temperature oxidation testing
  - *Falsified if:* If the predicted changes in microstructure and properties are not observed, or if the precipitation of intermetallic compounds does not correlate with the changes in corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance, the hypothesis is falsified.
