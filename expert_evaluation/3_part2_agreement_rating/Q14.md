## The research question (same as in Part 1)

> For a selected high-entropy alloy whose superconducting behavior remains uncharacterized, what governs whether and how superconductivity emerges in the low-temperature test regime, and why? Propose one testable mechanistic hypothesis, a controlled comparison with the starting state, the observations expected relative to that baseline, the limits over which the hypothesis should hold, supporting prior evidence, and a clear falsification criterion.

| **Key measurement** | The decisive comparison used the maxima in the imaginary AC susceptibility χ''(T), equal-entropy analysis of the heat-capacity anomaly, and WHH fits to Tc(H) derived from field-dependent specific heat; XRD/Rietveld and SEM-EDXS identified the structural phases mediating the difference. |
| **Actual result** | Both alloys showed bulk type-II superconductivity, but HEA-Tb had Tc = 4.6(1) K by heat capacity (about 4.7 K by susceptibility) versus 5.2(1) K for the matrix, and μ0Hc2(0) = 6.1 T versus 6.8 T. ## The three hypotheses (the same as in Part 1)

---

## Hypothesis A

- **Main hypothesis:** The emergence of superconductivity in the selected high-entropy alloy is governed by the alloy's electronic density of states and the formation of a coherent electronic state at low temperatures.
- **Starting material:** High-entropy alloy with unknown superconducting behavior
- **What is changed or studied:** Cooling to low temperatures
- **Conditions:** Low-temperature test regime
- **Structure through which it acts:** Electronic density of states
- **Mechanism:** Formation of a coherent electronic state
- **Predicted outcome:** Emergence of superconductivity
- **Compared with (baseline):** Non-superconducting state at high temperatures
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** Temperature range below the superconducting transition temperature (Tc)
- **Main uncertainty:** Insufficient data on the specific high-entropy alloy's composition and crystal structure
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Low-temperature transport measurements
  - *Measurements:* Electrical resistivity; Magnetic susceptibility
  - *Falsified if:* If no superconducting transition is observed below 10 K, the hypothesis is falsified

---

## Hypothesis B

- **Main hypothesis:** Thermal annealing enhances local compositional ordering in the high-entropy alloy, reducing lattice disorder and thereby promoting superconductivity by facilitating electron–phonon mediated Cooper pairing.
- **Starting material:** A homogeneously disordered high-entropy alloy with a random distribution of constituent elements, showing no or only weak signs of superconductivity at low temperatures.
- **What is changed or studied:** Apply a controlled thermal annealing treatment aimed at promoting short-range order without causing bulk phase separation.
- **Conditions:** Low-temperature testing (below the expected superconducting transition region) with parallel measurements on as-cast versus annealed samples under identical cryogenic and magnetic field settings.
- **Structure through which it acts:** Enhanced short-range or local compositional order (i.e., clustering or ordering of select elements) induced by the annealing process.
- **Mechanism:** Thermal treatment decreases lattice disorder, which reduces electron scattering and enhances specific phonon modes. These softer phonon modes more effectively mediate electron pairing, thereby facilitating the emergence of superconductivity in the alloy.
- **Predicted outcome:** Annealed samples will exhibit a distinct superconducting transition at a higher temperature, with sharper resistive and magnetic susceptibility transitions, compared to the disordered, as-cast baseline samples.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The untreated, disordered alloy that shows either no superconductivity or a very weak, broad transition under low temperature, characterized by a higher residual resistivity.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** This mechanism is expected to apply to high-entropy alloys where moderate annealing can enhance local ordering without inducing unwanted phase separation. The effect may not hold if the material experiences either too extreme annealing (leading to phase segregation) or if the intrinsic disorder is too severe to be remedied by thermal treatment.
- **Main uncertainty:** The precise annealing temperature and duration needed to achieve the optimal degree of local ordering remain undetermined, as does the exact magnitude of the improvement in the superconducting transition temperature.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Prepare two sets of identical high-entropy alloy samples. Subject one set to a controlled thermal annealing process aimed at increasing local compositional order while keeping the other set in the as-cast state. Perform low-temperature measurements including resistivity and magnetic susceptibility, as well as structural analysis (e.g., X-ray diffraction and TEM) to quantify ordering.
  - *Measurements:* Resistivity versus temperature curves; Magnetic susceptibility measurements versus temperature; Structural characterization (XRD, TEM) for assessing local order
  - *Falsified if:* If the annealed samples do not exhibit a significantly higher superconducting transition temperature or a sharper transition in resistivity and susceptibility than the as-cast samples, and if no increase in local ordering is detected through structural analysis, then the hypothesis that thermal annealing promotes superconductivity via enhanced local ordering is falsified.

## Hypothesis C

- **Main hypothesis:** Introducing Tb into the refractory high-entropy alloy (VNb)0.753(TiHf)0.247 will weaken rather than enhance bulk type-II superconductivity. Partial Tb incorporation and nonsuperconducting Tb-rich inclusions will broaden the transition, reduce the effective superconducting volume, and lower both Tc and the upper critical field.
- **Starting material:** The starting comparator will be as-cast polycrystalline (VNb)0.753(TiHf)0.247, expected to form a predominantly bcc matrix with slight Nb-rich/Nb-poor segregation and no XRD-detectable secondary phase.
- **What is changed or studied:** Tb will be introduced by arc melting to form nominal (VNb)0.67(TiTbHf)0.33, and this as-cast alloy will be compared directly with the Tb-free matrix.
- **Conditions:** Phase constitution and composition will be assessed by XRD/Rietveld analysis and SEM-EDXS. Zero-field-cooled AC susceptibility will be evaluated from about 1.8 to 6 K in fields up to approximately 5 T, while heat capacity will be evaluated down to 1.8 K in fields up to roughly 5–6 T. Field-dependent heat-capacity transitions will be analyzed with the dirty-limit WHH model.
- **Structure through which it acts:** The Tb alloy is expected to retain a bcc HEA matrix with a slightly enlarged lattice parameter of about 3.264 Å versus 3.257 Å, consistent with partial Tb incorporation. It will also contain approximately 3 wt.% metallic Tb, 2 wt.% Tb2O3, and a probable Tb concentration gradient near matrix/precipitate interfaces.
- **Mechanism:** Tb incorporation and Tb/Tb2O3 inclusions will create superconducting inhomogeneity and nonsuperconducting volume, weakening field robustness without providing meaningful flux-pinning enhancement. Suppression could arise from intrinsic Tb magnetism, altered electronic structure, or both, although bulk magnetic ordering is not expected within the test regime.
- **Predicted outcome:** The Tb-containing alloy should remain superconducting but show Tc near 4.6–4.7 K rather than about 5.2 K, μ0Hc2(0) near 6.1 T rather than 6.8 T, and a normalized heat-capacity jump near 1.1 rather than 1.5. Its transition should broaden, and superconductivity should disappear by about 5 T while remaining detectable in the reference. Electron–phonon coupling should decrease from roughly 0.68 to 0.62; orbital Hc2 should decrease from about 7.9 to 7.3 T, with little change in spin–orbit scattering near 1.1–1.2.
- **Compared with (baseline):** The Tb-free alloy should provide a sharp bulk transition near 5.2 K and remain superconducting at 5 T.
- **Where it should hold (boundary):** The hypothesis will apply to these as-cast polycrystalline compositions between 1.8 and 6 K and fields up to roughly 5–6 T; it will not generalize to every lanthanide addition or distinguish magnetic from electronic suppression.
- **Main uncertainty:** The principal uncertainty will be whether intrinsic Tb magnetism or Tb-induced electronic-structure modification dominates the degradation.
- **Test that would falsify it:**
  - *Design:* Compare phase-matched as-cast Tb-containing and Tb-free alloys across the same temperature and field ranges.
  - *Measurements:* χ''(T) maxima; equal-entropy heat-capacity Tc and normalized jump; WHH fits to Tc(H); XRD/Rietveld and SEM-EDXS phase analysis
  - *Falsified if:* The hypothesis will be falsified if Tb addition does not lower Tc or Hc2 and does not broaden or reduce the bulk heat-capacity and susceptibility response relative to the Tb-free baseline.
